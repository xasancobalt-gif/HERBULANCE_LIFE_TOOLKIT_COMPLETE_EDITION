Front Matter

Welcome to HERBULANCE Book 30

This is the first-aid and emergency-preparedness volume of the HERBULANCE Complete Life Toolkit. Its promise is deliberately modest and deliberately urgent: recognise danger, act safely, know what to do, prevent avoidable injuries, and prepare before emergencies happen.

The book is written for people with no medical background, in plain language, with everyday scenarios from homes, schools, offices, roads, workplaces and outdoors. It is internationally oriented — it tells you what to do, and it tells you where local rules, numbers and training may differ.

! Read this first — and keep it in mind forever

This book is educational. It is not a diagnosis, not a triage tool, and not a substitute for emergency services. In a real emergency, call your local emergency number first (112 in most of Europe, 911 in North America, 000 in Australia, 999 in the UK & Ireland, 112 in India — verify for your country). A dispatcher can talk you through the correct action while you are reading this book. Never delay a call to look up an answer.

✎ First-aid training limitation statement

Reading about CPR, choking, bleeding control or AED use does not make you a trained responder. First-aid skills are physical skills: hand position, depth, rhythm and judgement only become reliable with hands-on practice and feedback. A reader should only perform actions that are appropriate to their training, abilities, circumstances and current recognised guidance. Please take a practical first-aid and CPR course from a recognised provider (see the references section and your national Red Cross or equivalent body) — and refresh it periodically.

Part I

The Foundations of First Aid

Before any technique matters, five habits decide most outcomes: stay safe, understand the situation, know your limits, call early, and communicate well. This part builds those habits.

Part I · Chapter 1

Understanding First Aid and Why It Matters

  • Define first aid and its three goals.
  • Distinguish immediate care from definitive medical treatment.
  • Know what a bystander should and should not do.
  • Understand consent, dignity and your limits.

What first aid means

First aid is the immediate, appropriate care given to an injured or suddenly ill person before professional help arrives. It has three classic goals: preserve life, prevent the condition from worsening, and promote recovery. That is all — and it is a great deal.

Immediate care versus definitive treatment

A broken femur is not treated by a bystander — it is supported. Definitive treatment (surgery, medication, hospital monitoring) happens in a clinical setting. First aid bridges the gap: minutes, not hours, of correct simple action can change survival. Think of first aid as holding the line until the professionals arrive.

Roles: bystander, trained responder, professional

Three tiers of emergency response roles
RoleTypical actionsBoundaries
Bystander (anyone, untrained)Ensure scene safety, call emergency services, relay dispatcher instructions, give simple safe aid (direct pressure, cooling a burn, calming the person), bring an AED, guide responders in.Only actions you feel confident in and that are safe. You never owe an advanced manoeuvre — you owe a good call and a safe scene.
Trained first-aiderFull assessment, CPR with rescue breaths, advanced haemorrhage control, tourniquets, wound packing (where trained), anaphylaxis management, structured recovery position.Skills must be current (refresh every 1–2 years). Training is technique-specific — a CPR certificate does not make you a trauma specialist.
Professionals (EMS, doctors)Advanced airway management, drugs, surgical control of bleeding, spinal immobilisation, transport.They take over at handover. Never stop basic care at handover — tell them what happened and what you did.

Consent, dignity and privacy

A conscious, alert person can refuse care or treatment — respect that, note it, and call for help anyway. A person who is unconscious is generally treated under implied consent (the law presumes they would want life-saving care). Check ID wristbands or medical info cards, ask about allergies and medications, keep conversations private, and use a blanket or curtain for modesty — dignity is part of care.

Duty of care — a legal landscape, not a rulebook

Laws differ by country: some have no general duty to help; many have “Good Samaritan” protections that shield people who help in good faith and within their reasonable ability; workplaces and some professions (teachers, security) may carry specific duties. Two safe principles: never act beyond your competence, and never abandon a person once you have started helping. When in doubt, calling for help is almost always both legal and right.

Psychological support is first aid too

Your calm voice, clear instructions (“I've called 112, an ambulance is 8 minutes away — keep doing what I ask”) and steady presence reduce panic and improve recovery. Shouting, blame, or phone videos of the victim are not care. Chapter 30 covers psychological first aid (PFA) in depth.

✎Limits of first-aid knowledge

First aid is a floor, not a ceiling of medical care. It does not diagnose, it does not replace clinical judgement, and it has expiry dates — guidelines change (see the 2025 updates in Chapter 6). The most honest skill a first-aider has is knowing the edge of their own competence.

✕Myth · "If I mess up, I'll be in trouble"

Good Samaritan laws in most countries exist precisely to protect people who help reasonably and in good faith. Doing nothing is rarely legally safer than helping sensibly — and it may cost a life. Help within your ability, and call professionals without delay.

Key takeaways

  • First aid = immediate, appropriate care to preserve life, prevent worsening, support recovery.
  • You are the bridge between the accident and the hospital — the bridge, not the hospital.
  • Consent: ask when possible, assume yes for unconscious people, respect a clear refusal.
  • Your competence boundary is your responsibility — call early, act simply, hand over clearly.
Review questions
  1. Name the three goals of first aid.
  2. What can a completely untrained bystander do that is genuinely life-saving?
  3. What are the three pillars of the implied-consent and Good Samaritan principles?

1) Preserve life, prevent worsening, promote recovery. 2) Scene safety, calling emergency services, relaying dispatcher instructions, simple safe actions like pressure on bleeding or cooling a burn. 3) Act in good faith, within your reasonable ability, without abandoning the person.

Part I · Chapter 2

Scene Safety and Emergency Assessment

  • Scan for the main scene hazards before touching anyone.
  • Apply the DR sequence: Danger → Response.
  • Use simple protective barriers sensibly.
  • Report what you observe while waiting for help.

The one rule that outranks all techniques

Do not become a second casualty. In road collisions, electrical incidents, fires and chemical events, every year bystanders die or are seriously hurt by rushing in. The first “C” in CPR is not compressions — in practice it is check for danger. A quick 5-second sweep, eyes closed if light is dazzling, can save your life.

Hazard sweep — what to look for

🚗 Traffic & roads

Move to the shoulder, fit your seatbelt, switch on hazards, place a warning triangle 45–100 m back (more on motorways at night — see Ch. 24). Never help on the carriageway; move casualties to safety after the lane is controlled, and only if safe.

🔥 Fire & smoke

Smoke kills faster than flame. Do not enter smoke-filled spaces — close doors behind responders, evacuate, call fire services. Low crawling only if you must exit.

⚡ Electricity

Downed power lines energise the ground for metres. If a person is still in contact with the source, do not touch them. Shut off the source only if you can reach the switch in one safe step; otherwise mark the area and wait for the utility to de-energise.

☣ Chemicals & fumes

Approach from upwind. Strong smells, stinging eyes, or people vomiting all downwind indicate a release. Do not enter enclosed or low-lying areas (many vapours sink) without professional equipment.

🌊 Water

Drowning victims go under and grab rescuers. Throw, don't go: a rope, jacket or pole from stable ground. Only trained swimmers enter water, and only to make a rescue, not to start CPR in deep water.

🗡 Violence & unsafe people

Aggressive or armed persons, domestic violence, crowd scenes: create distance, call police first, and don't enter until the environment is declared safe. A well-aimed call beats a well-meaning entry.

Protection that actually helps

A pair of disposable nitrile gloves is the single most useful safety item in a kit. Breathing-barrier face shields (often built into CPR masks) protect you when giving rescue breaths. You do not need a hazmat suit for a kitchen cut — proportionate protection, and nothing if gloves make the task riskier (e.g., hands-on electrical shutoff).

Assessment once the scene is safe

  1. Look
    Coughing? Colour? Posture? Blood? Smoke? What happened (skid marks, spilled bottles, fallen trees) tells you a lot before you speak.
  2. Respond
    Kneel at chest height, speak clearly and tap the shoulders: “Can you hear me? Squeeze my fingers.” Any response — words, movement, breathing normally — means they are conscious.
  3. Breathe & call
    No response: call your emergency number now (2025 guidance emphasises the early call for any unresponsive person). Follow the dispatcher's instructions on checking breathing and starting CPR — Chapter 6.
  4. Observe while you wait
    Reassess response and breathing regularly, watch for colour changes, vomiting, worsening bleeding, and keep the person warm. Note the time of any change — dispatchers and paramedics will ask.
!Never, ever

…touch a person still in contact with electricity, walk through standing water on a downed line, enter a smoke-filled room “to check quickly”, approach a barking dog or an aggressive person to “see if they're hurt”, or enter any structure after an explosion or chemical smell. Professionals carry equipment you don't have — that is the point of the call.

✓Dispatcher-assisted first aid

Call and stay on the line. In most countries dispatchers can talk you through chest compressions, choking manoeuvres or wound pressure — this is called dispatcher-assisted CPR/first aid and it is remarkably effective even for total beginners. Turn your phone to speaker and put it against a soft surface.

Key takeaways

  • Scan before you step: traffic, fire/smoke, electricity, chemicals, water, violence.
  • If the hazard can't be made safe quickly, don't enter — call and control from a distance.
  • 5 seconds to look, then respond: kneel, speak, tap, ask.
  • Call early for any unresponsive person; let the dispatcher coach you.
Review questions
  1. A person has collapsed near a sparking cable. List your first three actions.
  2. What proportionate protective gear should a home kit contain, and why is it proportionate?
  3. Why do 2025 guidelines encourage calling before finishing the breathing check?

1) Do not touch the person or the cable; keep 8+ metres from downed lines; call emergency services and the utility; keep others back until the power is confirmed off. 2) Nitrile gloves and a CPR barrier — they cover the most common contact risks without slowing simple care. 3) Time matters more than a perfect check: a dispatcher confirms breathing with you while help is already en route.

Part I · Chapter 3

The Human Body During an Emergency

  • Understand airway, breathing, circulation and consciousness in plain language.
  • Recognise the signs of deterioration before you can measure anything.
  • Know why rapid assessment comes before detailed treatment.

The four vital systems, in plain language

The four vital systems in plain language
SystemWhat it doesWhat failure looks like
AirwayKeeps the passage from nose/mouth to lungs open.Gurgling or snoring in an unconscious person (tongue blocking), choking sounds, stridor (high-pitched noise on breathing in), no movement of chest.
BreathingMoves oxygen in, carbon dioxide out — normally 12–20 breaths/min at rest in adults.No chest movement, gasping/agonal breathing (rhythmic gasps that look like breaths but are not), very fast or laboured breathing, blue/grey lips or face.
CirculationPumps oxygenated blood everywhere (heart rate ~60–100/min at rest in adults).No pulse (only professionals confirm), severe bleeding, pale clammy skin, weak or absent pulse, very fast heartbeat, cold mottled limbs.
ConsciousnessBrain function: awake, alert, able to follow instructions.Drowsiness that won't lift, confusion, slurred speech, inability to wake, seizure, unresponsive.

These four interact constantly: the brain tolerates minutes without oxygen; the heart tolerates even less once it stops. That is why the assessment order is airway → breathing → circulation → response (neuro), and why we call before we linger on any single check.

Nerves, blood and heat — the supporting cast

  • Nervous system: carries everything — pain, movement, reflexes, the urge to breathe. Sudden weakness on one side, a blown pupil (unequal eye openings), or a seizure tells you the brain is in trouble (Ch. 10).
  • Blood and oxygen: blood carries oxygen, heat and your immune defence. Losing ~15% of blood volume produces pallor and fast pulse; more than ~30% produces shock (Ch. 8).
  • Temperature regulation: the body defends ~37 °C (98.6 °F). Fever, heatstroke and hypothermia are all this system failing — and the body's first defence in cold is to shiver, then to stop shivering (a danger sign).
  • Shock is a pattern, not a feeling: pale/cold/clammy skin, fast shallow breathing, fast weak pulse, confusion or agitation, thirst. Shock is a medical emergency even when the person “looks fine”. Never give food or drink to someone in shock.

Basic vital signs — concepts, not exams

You do not need to take a clinical pulse or count breaths like a nurse. What you do need is to notice: Is breathing normal? Is the person responsive? Is the skin pale, blue, grey, or hot-and-dry? Is bleeding continuing? Those four observations, reported to the dispatcher in plain words, are genuinely useful clinical information. Trained responders learn structured vital signs in courses; you learn change detection — “she was talking at 15:02, drowsy by 15:10” is a report that matters.

Signs of deterioration to act on immediately

!Call (or escalate the call) now if you notice

Worsening or no breathing · blue/grey lips or skin · loss of consciousness after any head injury · repeated vomiting · confusion getting worse · bleeding that soaks a dressing · seizure lasting more than 5 minutes · chest pain with sweating or breathlessness · hives plus any breathing trouble (anaphylaxis). If you already called, call back — it is normal and expected.

✎What this book will not teach you

Clinical examination skills (palpating pulses, auscultating, neurological scoring) belong to professional training. You will learn observation — which is safer, faster, and exactly what a dispatcher needs from you.

Key takeaways

  • Airway, breathing, circulation, response — the order of the assessment, and the order of the danger.
  • “Normal breathing” is quiet, regular, at a normal rate. Gasping is not normal breathing.
  • Report changes in plain language with times — you are a sensor, not a doctor.
  • Shock, blue lips, and deteriorating consciousness are always calls for escalation.
Review questions
  1. Why do gasping breaths (agonal breathing) count as not breathing?
  2. Name three observable signs of shock.
  3. What single observation question beats any home blood-pressure device?

1) They are the brainstem's dying reflex — oxygen is not getting to the body; CPR is indicated. 2) Pale/cold/clammy skin, fast shallow breathing, fast weak pulse, confusion/agitation, thirst. 3) “Is this the same as when I arrived?” — trend, not number.

Building a First-Aid Kit

  • Assemble a proportionate home kit.
  • Adapt kits for travel, vehicle, work, school and outdoors.
  • Run the monthly check that makes a kit real.
  • Keep contents safe from children and heat.

Principles before products

Fit, not fancy. A small kit you know and maintain beats a large one that expires. Include what you know how to use; add complexity only when you take the matching training course. Medications are a judgement, not a shopping list: different households need different medicines (asthma inhalers, prescribed adrenaline auto-injectors, diabetes supplies, personal regular medications). This book lists categories — your pharmacist or clinician tells you which apply to you.

Kit variants

Kit variants by context
KitEmphasise
TravelCompact pouch: plasters, antiseptic wipes, personal Rx meds, motion-sickness (if you use), thermometer, emergency contact card, small blanket.
VehicleGloves, dressings, bandage, shears, blanket, whistle, warning triangle/reflective vest (per local law), torch, power bank, contact card. Not in the boot where you'd have to open it in a live lane.
Workplace / schoolFollow local occupational-safety rules — larger kits, more gloves, trained responders on rota, AED nearby (Ch. 25).

The monthly 10-minute check

  1. Expiry dates — gloves, dressings, ointments, personal meds; replace what's expired (a home of medicine expiry is a fire hazard too).
  2. Stock levels — replace used plasters and dressings; one kit in, one kit out.
  3. Condition — no moisture, no crushed packaging, shears sharp, torch has fresh batteries (or charge the power bank).
  4. Placement & people — where is it? Does everyone in the house know? Is it locked away from curious toddlers (kits, like medicines, go on high shelves)?

Worksheet 4 (shopping checklist) and Worksheet 5 (inspection log) live in the Toolkit. Storage: cool, dry, dark, in a rigid box with a visible red cross — not the bathroom cupboard (humidity).

▲Child safety first

Any kit reachable by a child is a hazard: medications, sharp shears, and even a roll of adhesive can become a strangulation or ingestion risk. Store on a high shelf, lock the box, and treat the kit as a responder tool, not a toy.

✓The one device worth the training

If your budget supports one addition, it's not a gadget — it's a practical first-aid + CPR course for the adults in the home. A kit without a trained pair of hands is a shelf of expired plasters.

Key takeaways

  • Fit, not fancy: stock what you know how to use.
  • Medications and prescribed devices are personal decisions — ask your pharmacist/clinician.
  • Check monthly: expiry, stock, condition, placement.
  • Kits go high and out of children's reach; training goes on the adults.
Review questions
  1. Why is a monthly inspection more important than the initial purchase?
  2. Which two kit categories do all variants share, regardless of context?
  3. Name three reasons a vehicle kit should not live only in the boot.

1) First aid is time-sensitive and kits degrade: expired gloves, flat batteries, used-up dressings turn a good kit into a false sense of readiness. 2) Protection (gloves/barrier) and bleeding control (dressings/bandages). 3) Access time in an incident, theft/vandalism, and heat damage in many climates.

Part I · Chapter 5

Emergency Communication & Getting Help

  • Decide fast when a call is needed.
  • Deliver the core information clearly.
  • Follow dispatcher instructions to the letter.
  • Prepare for language barriers, travel and dead phones.

When to call — the short version

Call emergency services for anything RED-level in the front-matter warning guide: no normal breathing, unresponsiveness, severe bleeding, chest pain, stroke signs, anaphylaxis, major burns, suspected fracture of spine/long bone, head injury with any concerning sign, drowning, suspected poisoning/overdose, carbon monoxide exposure, and anything you are genuinely unsure about. When in doubt, call.

What the dispatcher needs from you

Core information to give a dispatcher
ItemWhy it matters
Exact locationAddress, town/district, nearest cross-street, landmark, floor/unit, which road you are on and which direction. For roads: kilometre markers or a visible landmark. Say it slowly; repeat if asked.
What happened“He collapsed while walking, no fall” vs “car vs pedestrian” changes which units arrive.
How many peopleDispatching scales with numbers. One badly injured person can outrank ten minor ones — the dispatcher decides.

Staying on the line

Never hang up first. Dispatchers may instruct CPR, bleeding control or choking manoeuvres in real time (dispatcher-assisted care). Put the phone on speaker, prop it up, and talk to the casualty while listening. If the line drops, call back immediately — the dispatcher is still watching your number.

Practical realities of a real call

  • Language barriers: ask for an interpreter service (many emergency centres offer them); otherwise point at the person, keep to single words, use the phone's translation app if you have it, or put a bilingual bystander on the phone.
  • While travelling: learn the local number before you leave; know your hotel/hostel name in the local script; carry your insurer's 24/7 assistance number; for road incidents, agree a visible meeting point if you must move for safety.
  • Dead battery or no signal: send a second person to the nearest road, shop or neighbour for help; flag or whistle signals; in vehicles, hazard lights; in remote areas, your pre-arranged check-in time is the alarm — tell someone your route and expected return before every remote outing (Ch. 24, 27).
  • Emergency information card: Worksheet 15 is your personal one-card summary — allergies, medications, blood type, conditions, contacts. Keep a copy in your wallet, your phone's notes, and give one to family.
✓Dispatcher communication template

“Emergency. [Address/landmark]. [What happened]. [Number of people]. The person is [conscious / unresponsive / not breathing / bleeding]. I can be reached on [number]. Hazard: [fire / electricity / none]. I'll follow your instructions.” Worksheet 22 turns this into a printable card.

▲Busy lines are normal

Do not give up on a busy signal — keep dialling, and if you reach voicemail or a general line, say “emergency” clearly. Neighbours, shops and taxi drivers all have phones. Calling twice (you + a bystander you point to) doubles the odds.

Key takeaways

  • Location, event, numbers, observations, your callback number, hazards — in that order.
  • Stay on the line; follow dispatcher instructions exactly; never hang up first.
  • Prepare for no-signal and language-barrier situations before you need them.
  • A written emergency information card beats a memory under stress.
Review questions

Part II · Chapter 6

Cardiac Arrest, CPR & AED Awareness

  • Recognise possible cardiac arrest in seconds.
  • Activate emergency services and an AED immediately.
  • Understand adult, child and infant CPR per 2025 guidance.
  • Know how AEDs protect both the patient and you.
!The 60-second version — learn this first

Person unresponsive + not breathing normally (or only gasping) = treat as cardiac arrest. ① Call emergency services now and put the phone on speaker — a dispatcher will coach you. ② Shout: “Who will get me the nearest AED?” ③ Start chest compressions hard and fast in the centre of the chest and continue until help takes over. Every minute without CPR roughly reduces survival — but any compressions beat no compressions, and AEDs in public buildings save lives every day.

Cardiac arrest vs heart attack

Heart attack: a coronary artery is blocked; the heart is still beating; the person is usually conscious, in chest pain, sweating, breathless. Cardiac arrest: the heart has stopped pumping; the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally. A heart attack can deteriorate into cardiac arrest in minutes — treat chest pain as an emergency (Ch. 9) and treat collapse as arrest.

Recognition

No response to voice or a firm shoulder tap, plus no normal breathing (absent, or only irregular gasps — agonal gasps are not breathing). Blue/grey lips and face. It can happen to anyone: young athletes, office workers, the elderly. If someone collapses suddenly with no obvious cause, assume cardiac arrest until told otherwise.

Adult CPR (16+ years)

  • Position: firm, flat surface. Kneel at the side, heel of one hand on the centre of the chest (lower half of the breastbone), other hand on top, fingers interlocked, arms straight over the hands.
  • Depth: 5–6 cm (2–2.4 in) — deep enough to matter, avoiding excessive depth.
  • Rate: 100–120 per minute, leaning with body weight.
  • Recoil: let the chest come fully up between compressions; don't lean on it.
  • Trained rescuers give 2 rescue breaths after 30 compressions (each breath ~1 second, watch for chest rise), minimising interruptions. Untrained bystanders: continuous hands-only CPR is strongly endorsed — call, then compress without stopping until the AED or EMS takes over.
  • Soft surfaces: 2025 guidance — start compressions immediately even on a bed or soft floor if moving is impractical or unsafe; a firm surface is preferable, not a prerequisite.
  • Ribs may crack — that is an accepted risk of life-saving compressions. Do not stop to avoid it.

Child CPR (1 year to before puberty)

  • Same recognition: unresponsive, not breathing normally. If the collapse is witnessed and sudden (likely cardiac), call first, then compress. If unwitnessed or suspected breathing-related (drowning, asthma, infection), one trained rescuer may give about 2 minutes of care before calling — in practice, if you are alone and unsure, call first.
  • Technique: heel of one hand, centre of the chest; depth about 5 cm — one-third of the chest's depth, not more than 6 cm; rate 100–120/min.
  • Ratios: alone: 30:2; two trained rescuers: 15:2. Trained rescuers give ventilations — especially important if the cause is likely respiratory (drowning, poisoning, infection).

Infant CPR (under 1 year)

  • Same recognition and call-first rule.
  • 2025 technique change: the two-thumb encircling-hands technique is the recommended method (two thumbs side by side on the lower half of the breastbone, hands encircling the chest, supporting the spine) — especially for two rescuers. For a solo lay rescuer, the heel of one hand is acceptable. The old two-finger method is no longer recommended.
  • Depth about 4 cm — one-third of the chest's depth; rate 100–120/min. Alone: 30:2; two trained rescuers: 15:2.
  • An AED may be used on infants — use a paediatric pad/mode if available; most infants will not be shocked because their rhythm is not shockable.

AED — automated external defibrillator

  1. Switch it on the moment it arrives (or even before opening, some units activate on lid-open). Follow the voice — it is written for total beginners.
  2. Attach pads to bare, dry chest exactly as the pictures on the pads show (upper right chest / lower left side for adults). Children may need a paediatric pad or mode; for very small children with adult pads only, front–back placement is an option taught in paediatric courses.
  3. Stand clear during analysis and any shock. “Everyone off the patient!” is a real sentence you will say.
  4. Resume compressions immediately after the shock decision, and keep going until EMS takes over or the person clearly recovers.
  5. Public access: look for the green sign with a heart and lightning bolt in airports, schools, offices, malls and transit hubs. Ask people to bring one while you start compressions.

When to stop CPR

Only when: professionals take over · the person starts moving/breathing normally · the AED directs a pause · the scene becomes unsafe · or you are physically unable to continue (exhaustion is real — swap rescuers if available). Do not stop because “it's been too long” — restarts have happened far beyond an hour.

Recovery position (breathing but unresponsive)

If the person is unresponsive but breathing normally, and not suspected to have a spine injury (see Ch. 14), turn them onto their side to keep the airway open and prevent vomit aspiration: support the head tilted back, pull the far arm across, bend the near knee, and roll them toward you. Re-check breathing regularly; if breathing stops, start CPR. (Trained courses teach the exact roll; the principle is side-lying with an open airway.)

Common myths

✕Myth · "I'm not trained, so I can't help"

You can help. Call, shout for an AED, and push hard and fast in the centre of the chest. Dispatcher-assisted CPR turns complete strangers into effective rescuers. Inaction is the only guaranteed failure.

✕Myth · "They'll be fine if we wait for the ambulance"

Without CPR and early defibrillation, survival falls roughly 7–10% for every minute that passes. Bystander CPR + public AED use is the single largest controllable factor in out-of-hospital survival.

✎Training limitation

This chapter teaches concepts. Compression depth, rhythm and breath technique are physical skills — take a hands-on course (any national Red Cross, St John Ambulance, heart-association or certified first-aid provider) and refresh every 1–2 years. Recording your course details on Worksheet 23 keeps you honest.

Key takeaways

  • Unresponsive + not breathing normally → call, send for AED, compress: centre of chest, 5–6 cm (adults), 100–120/min.
  • Untrained? Hands-only adult CPR is the guideline — do not wait for the “right” skill.
  • 2025: infant CPR uses two-thumb encircling (or heel of one hand solo); AEDs work on any age.
  • Trust the AED's voice; stand clear; resume compressions instantly after each shock decision.
  • Reading is not certification — book the practical course.

Choking & Airway Emergencies

  • Distinguish mild from severe obstruction instantly.
  • Apply the correct age-specific sequence (2025).
  • Know the infant rules that protect the baby.
  • Prevent the most common choking foods and objects.

Recognising choking

Mild (partial) obstruction: the person can cough forcefully, talk in short phrases, and move air — they are coughing something up. Severe (complete) obstruction: they cannot talk, cannot breathe in, cannot or barely can cough; voice is weak or absent; they may grab their throat (the universal choking sign); lips or face turn blue. Silent coughing or a high-pitched noise on breathing in are late and frightening signs.

Related airway situations

  • Swallowed a fish bone / foreign object but can swallow and breathe: this is not choking. Do not try home remedies (swallowing bread or rice, vinegar, oil). Seek urgent medical assessment — endoscopy is quick and safe.
  • Sudden stridor (noisy breathing in) in a child: could be a lodged object or croup; call emergency services if breathing is laboured or colour changes.
  • Allergic airway swelling (throat tightness, hives, voice change): that is anaphylaxis — Chapter 17, not a choking sequence.

Prevention — most choking never needs to happen

Food

Cut grapes, cherry tomatoes, hard candy and nuts into small pieces; cook carrots and celery until soft for young children; supervise every meal; no food while running, laughing or in the car; sit upright to eat.

Children under 5

Keep away: whole nuts, popcorn, hard/candy sweets, balloons (burst balloons are a classic fatal swallow), coins, button batteries (also a corrosive ingestion — always urgent), small toy parts. Check the age rating; check the carpet weekly for lost small objects.

Older adults

Choking risk rises with age, dental problems, swallowing difficulty after stroke, and sedating medication. Soft-textured food, upright posture, no talking through a mouthful, and a clinician-reviewed swallowing assessment after any stroke (see Ch. 23).

✕Myth · "The Heimlich is one big push"

Current guidance is alternating 5 back blows and 5 thrusts (adults/older children), reassessing after each cycle. One huge push is not the technique — and the “back blows first” step is often skipped in training videos.

✓Learn it standing up

Choking sequences are posture skills. In your first practical course, do them on a manikin with a partner spotting — and know that the dispatcher will talk you through the exact sequence live if you freeze. That is what they are trained to do.

Key takeaways

  • Can cough and talk? Encourage coughing — don't intervene.
  • Severe: 5 back blows → 5 abdominal thrusts (adults/children if trained), alternating, call for help.
  • Infants: 5 back blows → 5 chest compressions; never abdominal thrusts.
  • Unresponsive → CPR, look (never sweep) before breaths, AED if available.
  • Prevention is the best first aid: small pieces, supervision, no small objects under age 4.

Part II · Chapter 8

Severe Bleeding, Shock & Circulation

  • Control most bleeding with direct pressure, done correctly.
  • Recognise the small number of wounds that need a tourniquet.
  • Recognise and support a person in shock.
  • Know the “never do” list for traumatic bleeding.

The common case: direct pressure

  1. Protect yourself — gloves if available; a plastic bag over your hand is a legitimate barrier.
  2. Press — a sterile dressing or clean cloth directly on the wound, with steady firm pressure, using your whole palm. Keep pressing for a full 10 minutes; do not lift to “check”.
  3. Layer, don't replace — if blood soaks through, add a dressing on top and keep pressing. Removing the soaked dressing removes the forming clot.
  4. Elevate the limb if there is no suspected fracture and it doesn't increase pain.
  5. Watch the person, not just the wound: pallor, fast breathing, confusion → shock below. Keep them lying down and warm.

The rare case: severe limb bleeding

Tourniquet awareness. For a limb wound that is spurting, or so heavy that it soaks dressings in minutes or fills a container — and only when direct pressure is insufficient — a tourniquet is the correct tool. This is a RED emergency in every case: call while applying.

  • Use a commercial tourniquet if one exists (some outdoor kits carry them). An improvised one must be a wide strip — at least 5 cm / 2 in (belt, scarf, torn shirt). Never wire, string or thin cord — they cut tissue.
  • Apply 5–7 cm above the wound, not over a joint. Tighten until the bleeding stops. Note the time on a visible spot (forehead of the person or the band itself — EMS will ask).
  • Do not loosen or remove it, do not cover it, do not add a second one. The hospital manages it. A correctly applied tourniquet's risks are far smaller than exsanguination.

Wound-packing awareness: for deep wounds where pressure cannot reach the bleeding point — groin, armpit, neck — trained responders pack the wound with gauze and apply deep pressure. If you are not trained, give the best direct pressure you can, keep it on, and keep calling. Never probe into a wound with random objects.

Special wounds

Objects stuck in the body

Knives, glass, rebar: do not remove them. Dress around the object, hold it steady, call emergency services. Removal in the field can restart the bleeding the object is plugging.

Head wounds

Firm pressure with a dressing; a helmet stays on unless breathing is obstructed (a trained decision). Dents in the skull, fluid or blood from ears/nose, or any loss of consciousness → emergency (Ch. 14).

Nosebleeds

Sit upright, lean forward, pinch the soft part of the nose for 10 full minutes. Lying back or tilting the head up runs blood down the throat. Recurrent or post-injury nosebleeds → medical assessment.

Internal bleeding (awareness)

No visible wound but: pale/clammy, fast weak pulse, confusion, a swollen or rigid abdomen, blood in vomit (red or coffee-ground), black tarry stools, or blood in urine → emergency. These are shock signs with a hidden source.

Shock: recognise and support

Shock = the circulation can no longer supply the body. Signs: pale/cold/clammy skin, fast shallow breathing, fast weak pulse, agitation or confusion, thirst, drowsiness. It follows major trauma, blood loss, severe infection, heart attack, and anaphylaxis — any shock is an emergency call.

  1. Call emergency services (if not already called).
  2. Lay the person down; if possible and comfortable, raise the legs ~30 cm (no suspected leg/spine injury). If they may vomit, use the recovery position.
  3. Keep them warm — blanket, jacket; shock cools the body, and cooling worsens it.
  4. Nothing by mouth — no water, food or medication; they may need surgery and could vomit.
  5. Reassess continuously — response and breathing — and hand over exactly what you observed and when.
!Never

…remove a soaked dressing to inspect the wound · use ice packs directly on a bleeding wound to “close vessels” (it doesn't work and delays pressure) · submerge a bleeding limb in a basin of water (the clot washes away) · remove embedded objects · apply tourniquets to ordinary cuts (they are for life-threatening limb bleeding) · give food, drink or painkillers to someone in shock.

✕Myth · "Pinch the pressure points"

Arterial “pressure points” are a trained-responder technique for specific sites; for a bystander, direct pressure on the wound is faster, safer and more effective. Press the wound, not the anatomy textbook.

Key takeaways

  • Firm, continuous direct pressure on the wound for 10 minutes; layer dressings, never lift.
  • Tourniquet = life-threatening limb bleeding only; wide strip, 5–7 cm above, time noted, never loosened.
  • Embedded objects stay in; dress around them.
  • Shock: call, lay flat, warm, nothing by mouth, monitor.
  • When the bleeding is beyond your dressings, you are a dispatcher, a warmer and a witness — that is a complete job.
Review questions
  1. Blood soaks through your dressing after 8 minutes. What do you do — and what must you not do?
  2. Which three details do you record about a tourniquet for the paramedics?
  3. Name the four support steps for a person in shock.

1) Add a second dressing on top and keep pressing — never remove or lift the first one, never submerge it in water. 2) Time of application, where it was applied, and which limb. 3) Call, lay flat (legs raised if safe), keep warm, nothing by mouth — plus continuous monitoring.

Heart Attack & Chest Pain

  • Recognise heart-attack symptoms — including the atypical ones.
  • Call emergency services without negotiating with the symptoms.
  • Support the person safely while you wait.
  • Understand when medication decisions are not yours to make.

Classic symptoms

Pressure, squeezing, heaviness or pain in the centre of the chest lasting more than a few minutes, or coming and going; spreading to the left or both arms, neck, jaw or back; with sweating, nausea, breathlessness, or a cold fear. It does not look like the dramatic TV collapse. People often stop what they are doing and sit down, looking pale and worried.

Atypical presentations — the part people miss

▲Women, older adults & diabetes

Women are more likely to present with fatigue, nausea, shortness of breath, back or jaw pain without classic chest crushing. Older adults and people with long-standing diabetes may have few or no pain signals at all. If any of these patterns appears suddenly in someone at risk, it is a heart attack until proven otherwise — not “just indigestion”, “just stress”, “just age”.

What to do

  1. Call emergency services — describe the symptoms and location. The ambulance can begin treatment en route, which out-hospitals cannot do.
  2. Stop all activity. Have the person sit down, leaning slightly forward, legs relaxed. Loosen tight clothing (collar, belt). Keep them warm and as calm as you can be calm.
  3. Stay with them and keep talking. Follow the dispatcher's instructions exactly — including anything about medication.
  4. Medication — the dispatcher decides. In some places the dispatcher will tell you to have the person chew an aspirin; in others not. Give it only if instructed, the person is fully alert and can swallow, and they have no known allergy, active bleeding, or recent surgery/bleeding disorder. If the person has a prescribed heart medication (e.g., nitroglycerin) they normally use for angina, help them take it as they would. Never give aspirin as a universal reflex for any chest pain — the person may be bleeding internally, or it may be a stroke (Ch. 10) where aspirin is the wrong answer.
  5. If they collapse and stop breathing normally → switch to cardiac arrest response: CPR + AED. The heart attack just became an arrest; the sequence is your friend.
  6. Hand over everything: what, when, symptoms, medication given, and your observations of changes.

While you wait — the small things that matter

  • Do not let them walk, drive, “test” the pain by exertion, or take a hot shower (all raise cardiac demand).
  • Unlock the front door and clear a path for the crew; find their medication bag and ID.
  • Reassess every few minutes: response, breathing, colour. If they worsen, call back — re-calling is expected and useful.
  • Pet or child with the same symptoms? Vets treat animal cardiac events too — the early-call principle is the same.
✕Myth · "It's probably gas — he ate spicy food"

Heart attacks are repeatedly mistaken for indigestion because they feel like indigestion. The difference is time and pattern: new, central, pressing, minutes-long, with sweat or breathlessness — that is an ambulance, not antacid. You do not need to be certain; the hospital's ECG settles it in minutes.

✕Myth · "Young people don't have heart attacks"

They do — from exertion, from congenital conditions, from stimulants. Sudden collapse during sport is treated as cardiac arrest (Ch. 6) until proven otherwise.

Key takeaways

  • New central chest pressure/pain lasting minutes = emergency services, every time.
  • Atypical patterns (women, older adults, diabetes): fatigue, jaw/back pain, breathlessness — same urgency.
  • Sit, lean forward, loosen, calm, warm, stay, follow the dispatcher.
  • Medication is a dispatcher/clinician decision — not a home reflex.
  • Collapsed + not breathing → CPR and AED without a pause.
Review questions
  1. Your 62-year-old mother reports “terrible fatigue and a pain in my jaw and upper back” for an hour. Walk through your response.
  2. Why is aspirin a dispatcher decision rather than a first-aid default?

Stroke & Neurological Emergencies

  • Apply FAST (and friends) in under a minute.
  • Record the time of onset — it changes treatment.
  • Manage seizures safely and without the classic errors.
  • Handle fainting, confusion and “worst headache of life” correctly.

Stroke: the time-critical emergency

A stroke is the brain losing blood supply (ischaemic — a clot, ~85%) or a brain vessel bursting (haemorrhagic). You cannot tell which from the outside — and you don't need to. Both are emergencies, and the ischaemic type is treatable with clot-busting or clot-removal that only works within a time window. That is why the last letter of FAST is the most important.

Stroke response

  1. Call emergency services and say “suspected stroke” plus the time symptoms started. That word and that number route them to the right hospital fast.
  2. Keep them safe and still. Sitting or lying in a supported position; do not give food, drink or any medication (swallowing may be impaired; if it's a haemorrhagic stroke, antiplatelets would be the wrong drug).
  3. Note and relay changes — “left arm started at 14:20, speech got worse at 14:35”. If they vomit, recovery position. If breathing stops, CPR (Ch. 6).
  4. Symptoms that come and go (“mini-stroke”/TIA): still an emergency. A TIA is the system's one loud warning before a major stroke.
!Sudden “worst headache of your life”

A thunderclap headache — maximal intensity within seconds, often “like being hit” — with or without neck stiffness, vomiting or reduced consciousness is treated as a possible haemorrhagic stroke: emergency services now. So is any headache with new weakness, confusion or seizure.

Seizures

Generalised convulsive seizure: loss of consciousness, rhythmic jerking of the body, sometimes biting the tongue, incontinence, then drowsiness. Typical duration 1–3 minutes.

  1. Protect, don't restrain. Clear sharp and hard objects; cushion the head with a jacket. Do not hold the person down, and do not put anything in their mouth — nothing. Myths about “swallowing the tongue” are wrong; the tongue does not go down the throat.
  2. Time the seizure. A phone timer. This single number guides every later decision.
  3. After it ends: recovery position (protection from vomit), quiet, dim, no food or drink until fully alert, calm presence (Ch. 30: PFA — seizures are frightening for witnesses).
  4. Call emergency services if: the seizure lasts more than 5 minutes · a first-ever seizure · seizures back-to-back without recovery · injury during the seizure · pregnancy · diabetes · in water · or they don't return to normal.

Fainting (syncope) and reduced consciousness

  • Simple faint: warning signs (tunnel vision, cold sweat), brief loss of consciousness, quick full recovery. Lay flat, raise legs, fresh air, remove crowd. Still worth medical assessment if it's the first one, happened during exertion, with chest pain, in pregnancy, or with a head knock.
  • Not a simple faint: no quick recovery, confusion beyond 15–30 minutes, seizure-like activity, head injury, chest pain, or the person is an older adult (fainting in later life has more serious causes) → emergency or urgent assessment.
  • Unexplained confusion: can be stroke, low blood sugar (Ch. 20), head injury, severe infection (especially in older adults), or poisoning. “Something is not right with them” + confusion = call for advice or help; when in doubt, call.
✎Action boundary

For seizures and stroke you do positioning, timing and reporting — not medical management. For fainting you do positioning and observation. The clinical decisions (imaging, clot-busters, anti-epileptics) belong to the hospital. Your 5-minute timer and 2-minute FAST are genuinely the most valuable inputs the emergency team will get outside the ambulance.

✕Myth · "Hold their tongue / put a spoon in their mouth"

Nothing goes in the mouth of a seizing person — you can break teeth, block the airway, or get bitten, and the feared tongue-swallowing does not happen. Space, time, recovery position. That is the whole technique.

Key takeaways

  • Face · Arms · Speech · Time — sudden neurological change is a stroke until proven otherwise, and TIAs are emergencies too.
  • Time of onset is a clinical fact — record and report it.
  • Stroke: no food, no drink, no medication, stay with them, call “suspected stroke + time”.
  • Seizure: protect the head, don't restrain, nothing in the mouth, time it, recovery position after.
  • Thunderclap headache = emergency. Fainting with red flags = not “just a faint”.

The everyday injuries that fill first-aid kits: most are manageable with calm, correct, simple care — and the minority that aren't are recognisable. The skill is telling which is which.

Part III · Chapter 11

Cuts, Wounds & Skin Injuries

  • Clean and close minor wounds correctly.
  • Recognise wounds that need professional treatment.
  • Spot the early signs of infection.
  • Understand tetanus awareness.

The common path: small clean cuts & grazes

  1. Wash your hands (or wear gloves) before touching the wound.
  2. Stop the bleeding — light pressure with a clean cloth; most small wounds stop within minutes.
  3. Rinse the wound under clean running water for a few minutes. Saline is better; plain water is fine. Gently work out grit; don't scrub tissue, and don't dig for deep fragments.
  4. Close — small clean edges: a sterile strip (adhesive plaster) or, within about 6–8 hours and for clean straight edges, a skin-closure strip. Larger gaps need professional closure (stitches can look best for up to ~12–24 hours depending on location, but the sooner, the better).
  5. Protect — a plain dressing over a thin layer of plain antiseptic is enough. Don't pack wounds with homemade remedies, ointments you can't explain, or alcohol straight into the wound (it damages tissue and burns).
  6. Review daily, keep it clean and dry-ish, change the dressing if wet or dirty.

Infection warning signs (watch days, not minutes)

Increasing redness (especially red streaking), warmth, swelling, pus, worsening pain after it should be improving, fever, or redness spreading after day 2–3. Any of these → medical assessment. Tetanus awareness

Tetanus is a vaccine-preventable infection of deep or dirty wounds. You don't treat it — you prevent it: your primary series (childhood) plus boosters every ~10 years, reviewed after any dirty/deep wound. If you're unsure of your status and the wound is dirty or deep, ask a clinician about a booster. This is a doctor's call, not a first-aid one — but asking is your job.

✕Myth · "Let the wound breathe / put iodine, alcohol, turmeric or soil in it"

Unpacking household remedies into wounds adds more risk than the wound had. Clean water, plain antiseptic, a breathable dressing, and professional review when indicated — that's the whole evidence-based protocol.

Key takeaways

  • Rinse, protect, review daily — the 3 R's of minor wounds.
  • Depth, contamination, bites, face/hands, and “it won't close” are the professional-care triggers.
  • Dirty punctures raise a tetanus question — that's a clinician's answer, not a home guess.
  • Keep wounds clean and covered; keep remedies out.

Part III · Chapter 12

Burns & Scalds

  • Apply the cooling standard correctly.
  • Grade burn severity and route to the right care.
  • Handle chemical and electrical burns safely.
  • Know the household remedies that must never touch a burn.

The one technique that works: cool it

Cool the burn under cool (not icy) running water for 20 minutes — or with cool clean compresses if water isn't there. This limits tissue damage, relieves pain, and reduces swelling. Start immediately; 20 minutes is the standard in current first-aid guidance (WHO and national first-aid bodies). Remove rings, watches and non-stuck clothing while cooling — swollen skin makes removal later very painful.

After cooling: cover

Loosely cover with cling film (food wrap) or a clean, non-fluffy dressing — it keeps the wound clean, reduces pain, and doesn't stick. Blister? Don't pop it — intact skin is the best infection barrier. Leave hair, dressings and stuck clothing alone (cut around, don't pull).

Special burns

Chemical burns

Flush with plenty of water for at least 20 minutes, removing contaminated clothing as you go. Do not try to neutralise the chemical with lemon, bicarbonate or other household “antidotes” — the reaction generates heat and adds a chemical burn on top of the original one. Dry powder chemicals: brush off before wetting (some react violently with water) — wear gloves/protect yourself, then flush. Know the substance (label/phone) and tell the hospital.

Electrical burns

Make the source safe first (Ch. 2) — never touch the person while energised. Even a small entry point can hide deep internal damage, and electrical injury can trigger cardiac arrest later: monitor breathing, call emergency services for anything more than a trivial contact, and watch for delayed collapse.

Sunburn

Cool with damp cloths, moisturise, drink water, pain relief per label. Peeling + fever + feeling ill → medical assessment. Sunburn is preventable (Ch. 19) — and severe childhood sunburns raise lifetime skin-cancer risk.

Children

Burns cover a smaller proportion of a child's body area, so the RED thresholds are lower: any burn to a child's face, hands, feet, genitals, or across a joint, or anything larger than the child's own palm, gets urgent professional assessment. Scalds (hot water in bath/pots) are the common child burn — the prevention chapter is Ch. 22.

!Never on a burn

Ice or iced water (it damages tissue further) · butter, oil, toothpaste, egg white, flour, “grandma's ointments” (they trap heat and invite infection, and make professional cleaning much harder) · popping blisters · pulling off stuck clothing. The full list of folk remedies is long; the evidence-based list is two lines: cool water 20 minutes, cover, and call when indicated.

✓Large burns: the person, not the wound

Major burns cause shock and hypothermia: keep the person warm (blankets, but don't overheat), keep them lying down, nothing by mouth, and manage the bystander crowd — panic and well-meaning onlookers are part of the incident.

Key takeaways

  • Cool 20 minutes under running (or cool compress) water. Then cover with cling film or a clean non-fluffy dressing.
  • Blister stays on. Stuck clothing stays on (cut around it). Rings come off early.
  • Chemical: remove, brush off dry powders, then flush 20 minutes. Electrical: safe source first, always monitor.
  • Face, hands, genitals, joints, large area, full-thickness, or any child's significant burn = professional care, early.
  • No ice. No butter. No toothpaste. Ever.
Review questions
  1. Why is a “painless” burn more concerning than a painful one?
  2. A child's hand gets a scald in the bath. Sequence your response.
  3. Why do you brush off dry chemical powders before wetting the skin?

1) Painless means the nerves are destroyed — full-thickness injury, deeper damage, higher risk. 2) Stop the water flow/source, cool the hand under cool running water for 20 minutes removing the wristwatch while cooling, cover with cling film, call for urgent assessment (hand + child = professional care), keep the child calm and warm. 3) Many dry chemicals (lime, some metals, concentrated salts) react exothermically with water — wetting first adds a chemical burn on top of the mechanical one.

Part III · Chapter 13

Fractures, Sprains, Strains & Dislocations

  • Tell a likely fracture from a bad sprain — and accept that you may not be able to.
  • Support and immobilise without causing harm.
  • Know when the ambulance is the right call.
  • Apply RICE for the common soft-tissue injuries.

Recognition (and its limits)

Pointed features: obvious deformity, a limb that's the wrong shape or length, grating sensation (crepitus), inability to bear weight or use the limb, rapid swelling, severe pain on movement. But a “stable”-looking injury can be a fracture, and a very swollen sprain can mimic one. The honest rule: treat it as a fracture until a clinician says otherwise. You do not need to diagnose — you need to not make it worse.

Fracture response

  1. Stop the activity and the movement. “Don't move it” is the correct first instruction, said calmly.
  2. Control any bleeding first (an open fracture bleeds; pressure around the wound, never on exposed bone, and never push anything back in).
  3. Support it in the position found. You do not straighten, pull, or “set” anything. Pad and rest the limb on cushions, a bag, a rolled jacket — whatever is at hand — at the position of least pain. A sling for arms (a shirt works); for a leg, tie it loosely to the un-injured leg with a folded cloth padding if a proper splint isn't available — loose enough to slide a finger under, checking toes/fingers for colour and feeling above and below.
  4. Cold pack (cloth-wrapped — never ice directly on skin) over the swelling, 15–20 minutes at a time.
  5. Decide the route: obvious deformity, open wound with bone visible, head/neck/spine involvement, a limb that's cold/pale/numb beyond the injury, a hip injury in an older adult (fall from standing height), or any pain/loss of function that won't settle → emergency services or urgent care. A clean, well-supported sprain-like injury in an alert adult can often be a supervised ride to an assessment — but “often” is not “always”; when unsure, call.
!Circulation check you must do

After any splinting, check the fingers/toes beyond the support: colour (pink), warmth, and that the person can feel a light touch. Blue, cold, or numb = your support is too tight or the injury is compromising the limb — loosen and re-check, and treat as an emergency.

Dislocations

A joint that's visibly out of place (shoulder, finger, jaw) with pain and inability to move. Do not attempt to push it back — untrained reduction can tear vessels and nerves. Support it as found (sling for shoulder/finger; for a jaw, no manipulation at all), cold pack, and professional care — repositioning is a clinic procedure with imaging.

Sprains & strains (the common 90%)

Twisted ankle, pulled hamstring: pain + swelling without deformity. The current guidance is PEACE & LOVE (modern replacement of RICE where taught):

  • Days 1–3 — PEACE: Protect (avoid painful use, 2–3 days, don't fully immobilise) · Avoid anti-inflammatories in the first 48 h per current guidance (they can slow early healing) — paracetamol/acetaminophen for pain if needed · Compress (elastic bandage) · Elevate above heart level where practical · Educate: some swelling is part of healing; reassure.
  • From day 3 — LOVE: Loading (gradual, pain-guided movement — early gentle motion beats prolonged rest) · Optimism (recovery is the expected path) · Vascularisation (aerobic activity as tolerated) · Exercise (progressive strengthening — this is where real recovery happens, ideally guided by a physiotherapist for bigger injuries).
  • Escalate to professional care: can't bear weight at all, deformity, rapid large swelling, numbness, no improvement in a week, or any injury in a person on blood thinners or with bone conditions.
Related HERBULANCE volume: Book 24 — Bone & Joint Health covers the prevention side: bone density, joint care, strength work and fall-proofing that keep these injuries from happening in the first place.

Key takeaways

  • Support in the position found; never straighten, pull, or reduce.
  • Circulation check above and below any support, every time.
  • Open fractures, head/neck involvement, hip falls in older adults, “cold/blue beyond” = emergency.
  • Soft tissue: PEACE & LOVE — protect briefly, then load, strengthen, recover.
Review questions
  1. Why is “support in the position found” the core of lay fracture care?
  2. Your colleague's ankle swells fast after a twist, no deformity, they can take two careful steps. Route?
  3. What circulation check follows every splint you make?

1) Because alignment, bleeding and nerve damage are already set by the injury; moving it risks worsening all three, while support prevents the bystander from becoming the cause of secondary damage. 2) Amber: PEACE & LOVE at home, but rapid swelling + weight-bearing difficulty warrants medical/physio assessment within a day or two to rule out fracture. 3) Colour, warmth, and light-touch sensation of the fingers/toes distal to the support — rechecked after any adjustment.

Part III · Chapter 14

Head, Neck & Spinal Injuries

  • Recognise concussion and its danger window.
  • Decide when a head injury is an emergency.
  • Understand the limited, evidence-based role of bystanders in suspected spinal injury.
  • Know what to monitor and who decides return-to-play.

Head injury & concussion

Concussion = a functional brain injury from impact or jolt: confusion, disorientation, dizziness, headache, nausea, sensitivity to light/noise, amnesia around the event, irritability, worse with mental effort. It can be subtle — “they seemed a bit off” is a real observation.

Head injury: emergency red flags

!Call emergency services after a head knock if

Loss of consciousness (even seconds) · vomiting (especially repeated) · worsening or persistent headache · confusion, slurred speech, unusual drowsiness or difficulty waking · seizure · fluid or blood from ears or nose · a visible dent in the skull · unequal pupils · the person is over ~65, on a blood thinner, or the mechanism was serious (fall from height, road collision, cycling without helmet at speed). Watch the danger window: the serious complications (bleeding under the skull) declare themselves over the following 24–48 hours — anyone with a significant knock gets rest, a responsible adult checking them (waking them periodically in the first night per local advice), and no alcohol, no solo sleeping unsupervised, no return to sport until cleared.

Neck & spine: the bystander's narrow job

Suspected spinal injury: high-energy mechanism (road collision, fall from height, dive into shallow water, motorcycle crash) + neck pain, limb weakness/numbness, or loss of bladder/bowel control; or any unresponsive person from a high-energy mechanism.

  • Do not move them unless they are in immediate danger (fire, traffic, flood) — and if you must move, move the whole body as one unit with at least one other person, keeping head–neck–back aligned. This is the one exception to “don't move”, and it is a team action.
  • Do not try to “check” the spine by twisting, lifting the head, or making them “walk a bit to test it”.
  • Support the head gently in the position found (folded clothing on each side), reassure, control bleeding elsewhere, keep them warm, and keep the airway open without tilting the head back if spinal injury is suspected (use the jaw-lift if trained; if they aren't breathing and you're untrained, dispatcher will guide you — airway always beats alignment when breathing is absent).
  • The collar is the ambulance's job. Bystander “immobilisation” with random boards and straps has caused more harm than help; modern pre-hospital care uses minimal, trained immobilisation. Your job: hold the head, keep the scene quiet, tell responders the mechanism.

Falls, sport and the return-to-activity decision

After any concussion: no return to sport, driving, or risky work until cleared by a qualified clinician — and return is staged (gradual steps over days to weeks), not a single “feel fine” morning. A second concussion before the first has healed is far more dangerous than either alone. In children, watch for the same red flags plus irritability, poor feeding, and unusual sleep. The same rule applies at work and at school: the clearance comes from a professional, not a parent, coach or manager.

✎Action boundary

For the spine you do minimum movement, head support, airway (if needed), warmth, communication. You do not diagnose, image, or “realign”. For concussion you do rest, monitoring, red-flag vigilance, and a handover to the clinician who decides return to activity. That boundary is the entire technique.

✕Myth · "Keep them awake by not letting them sleep"

Current guidance: it's fine to let a concussed person sleep (rest aids healing) — what matters is the red flags. If local advice in your region says wake and check, follow it; what never changes is: red flags = emergency, and the first night gets a responsible watcher either way.

Key takeaways

  • Head injury + any red flag = emergency; the danger window runs 24–48 hours, not 24 minutes.
  • Suspected spinal injury: don't move unless in immediate danger; support the head; airway first if not breathing; let EMS immobilise.
  • Concussion recovery is clinical and staged — coaches and parents manage rest, clinicians decide return.
  • Blood thinners and age over 65 lower the threshold for “significant” to “emergency”.
Review questions
  1. A cyclist without a helmet falls at speed, is awake, complains of neck pain. Your first five actions?
  2. Why is “waking to check” still relevant even though sleep is allowed?
  3. What is the one situation where moving a spinal-injury suspect is correct?

1) Keep them still and calm, call emergency services, support the head in the position found, keep them warm, monitor breathing and response, control any external bleeding away from the neck — and don't let them “test walk”. 2) Because a deteriorating bleed announces itself through reduced response; a scheduled check catches deterioration that sleep would otherwise mask, and it keeps a responsible adult engaged with the patient. 3) Immediate danger to life — fire, traffic, flood — where the current risk exceeds the spinal risk, and the move is a coordinated team move keeping the body in line.

  • Water in the ear: tilt, pull the lobe, let it drain; a few days is normal; persistent blockage or pain → assessment (don't probe with cotton buds — they compact wax and cause injury).

Dental — the “2-hour tooth”

A knocked-out permanent tooth is time-critical and salvageable:

  1. Pick it up by the crown (the white part) — never the root; hold it gently.
  2. If dirty, rinse briefly (≤10 seconds) in milk or saline — not scrubbed, not scraped, not dried out.
  3. Reinsert if possible: clean hand, open the mouth, seat it in the socket, bite gently on a clean cloth to hold. If it won't go back in: store it in milk (best lay option), saliva (inside the cheek), or a tooth-preservation solution — never in water, never wrapped dry in a handkerchief.
  4. To a dentist or emergency department within the hour if at all possible — viability falls with every dry minute. A knocked-out milk tooth is never replanted (it would damage the developing permanent tooth): store it and get assessed anyway.
  • Broken tooth: collect the fragment (in milk), reduce pain with an over-the-counter painkiller per label + a cold pack on the cheek, seek dental care promptly (same day if there's pain, swelling, or a jagged edge near the gumline).
  • Severe dental pain/swelling: urgent dental care; facial swelling + fever + trouble swallowing or breathing is an emergency (dental infections can spread to the airway and neck).
  • Broken jaw (can't close the mouth, step in the bite, ear pain with jaw trauma): emergency services or emergency department; keep the airway open, soft or liquid food only, no heavy lifting.

  • Flush: water/saline, 20 minutes, lid held open — for grains and for chemicals (chemicals always get eye care after).

Part III · Chapter 16

Bites, Stings & Animal Injuries

  • Treat insect stings and bites safely.
  • Recognise the sting/bite that is actually anaphylaxis.
  • Handle animal and human bites with an infection-first mindset.
  • Respond to snakebite and marine stings without the folk remedies.

Insect stings & bites (the common case)

  1. Move away from the source (a bee colony, an ant trail) calmly.
  2. Remove the stinger (honeybee) quickly by scraping with a card or fingernail edge — don't squeeze the venom sac with tweezers if you can avoid it.
  3. Cold pack (cloth-wrapped) 10–20 minutes; an antihistamine or hydrocortisone cream per label eases the local reaction.
  4. Watch for the dangerous turn: swelling that spreads well beyond the sting site over an hour, widespread hives, itching of the mouth/throat, any breathing or voice change, dizziness → that's anaphylaxis, not a big local reaction. Adrenaline auto-injector if prescribed + emergency services, without delay.

Animal bites — infection is the story

  • Dog/cat bites: wash thoroughly with soap and running water for 10+ minutes (this single step measurably reduces infection), apply a plain antiseptic, cover, and seek same-day medical assessment — bites to hands, face, feet, joints and genitals have the highest risk, and children's faces especially. The clinician decides antibiotics and the tetanus question.
  • The animal's status matters: note (safely) whether the dog/cat is owned, vaccinated, and observable. Rabies awareness: in countries where rabies circulates (parts of South and Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa), a bite from any dog/cat/monkey — even a scratch that breaks skin — is a post-exposure prophylaxis decision to be made with a clinician promptly; it is not a “wait for symptoms” situation (rabies symptoms, once they appear, are almost universally fatal, which is exactly why the vaccine-before-symptoms window exists and must be used).
  • Human bites: the “clenched-fist injury” (punch to a mouth) is one of the most infection-prone wounds in medicine — same-day assessment, not a shower and a shrug. Kissing-bites on thumbs are the same story.
  • Jaw/face fractures from a bite or fight: emergency services (airway, bleeding, structure).

Snakebite — the do-nothing protocol

!Snakebite: safe response (all regions)

① Move the person away from the snake; keep them calm and still (movement pumps venom). ② Call emergency services / poison advice now and note the time of the bite; identify the snake from a distance if safe — a photo helps antivenom selection, but never risk a second bite for it. ③ Immobilise the bitten limb (sling or splint) and keep it at or slightly below heart level. ④ Remove rings, watches and tight clothing from that limb before swelling starts. ⑤ Mark the swelling edge with a pen and the time — clinicians track its spread. ⑥ Keep the person lying down, reassured, and moving to hospital as directed. Never: cut the wound, suck out venom, apply ice, apply a tight tourniquet or “pressure bandage” beyond trained regional protocol, give alcohol or sedatives, or chase the snake. A “pressure immobilisation bandage” is taught in specific snakebite regions (e.g., Australian elapids) — it is region- and species-specific, so follow local official guidance and the poison-centre's live instructions rather than a generic rule.

Scorpion, marine stings, jellyfish

  • Scorpion: remove the stinger, cold pack, pain relief per label; severe pain, spreading numbness, breathing difficulty, or a child under 5 → emergency services (some species' venom is genuinely dangerous; regional guidance varies).
  • Jellyfish: rinse the area with vinegar where local guidance says so (many marine-sting regions recommend it for boxjelly/Portuguese-man-of-war; some regions use seawater — follow your destination's marine-sting card), remove tentacles with tweezers or a credit-card edge (not bare fingers), hot-water soak (40–45 °C, test on your own skin) where recommended for pain. Any sting with breathing difficulty, large-area involvement, or stings to the face/mouth → emergency services.
  • Stonefish/sea urchin: remove visible spines with clean tweezers, hot-water soak 30–40 minutes for stonefish pain (well-established), medical assessment for deep spines, face/hand spines, or infection signs.
  • Shark / large marine animal: treat as major trauma — severe bleeding protocol (Ch. 8), emergency services, nothing heroic in the water (Ch. 2).
✕Myth · "Cut, suck, burn, tie off"

Every classic snakebite folk remedy is either useless or harmful: cutting and sucking moves venom into the rescuer's mouth and infects the wound; ice and tourniquets concentrate venom and destroy tissue; burning the site adds a burn. The evidence-based protocol is calm, still, immobilised, rings off, and fast professional care. Venom goes to the hospital, not the internet.

✓Regionalise before you travel

Snake, jellyfish, and scorpion guidance is genuinely different between Queensland, Kerala, California and the Caribbean. Before any trip with barefoot possibility: get the destination's official marine-sting and snakebite card (national health websites and dive shops both publish them) and put it in the travel kit (Worksheet 11).

Key takeaways

  • Sting: scrape the stinger, cold pack, watch for the anaphylaxis turn (breathing, throat, widespread hives, dizziness).
  • Bites: 10-minute soap-and-water wash, then same-day assessment — infection is the disease, not the pain.
  • Rabies-endemic regions: any dog/cat bite or skin-breaking scratch = prompt clinician contact about prophylaxis.
  • Snakebite: calm, still, immobilise, rings off, call, hospital. No cutting, sucking, icing, or tight ties.
  • Marine stings: follow the destination's card; breathing trouble or face/mouth involvement is always an emergency.
Review questions
  1. Why is the 10-minute soap-and-water wash the single most important first aid for a dog bite?
  2. List four snakebite actions that are forbidden and one that is required.
  3. When does a “local” bee-sting reaction become an emergency?

1) It mechanically dilutes and removes the bacterial load (including the species that cause the feared deep-hand infections) before it establishes — studies show it materially cuts infection rates; antibiotics treat what the wash prevents. 2) Forbidden: cutting the wound, suctioning, ice, tight tourniquet, alcohol. Required: immobilising the bitten limb and removing rings before swelling. 3) It stops being local: swelling spreading well beyond the site over the hour, widespread hives, mouth/throat itch or voice change, dizziness, any breathing difficulty — then it's anaphylaxis until proven otherwise.

Medical & Environmental Emergencies

Four conditions where recognition beats treatment: anaphylaxis, poisoning, heat/cold extremes, and the sudden internal problems — low sugar, asthma, severe infection — that announce themselves in patterns.

Part IV · Chapter 17

Severe Allergic Reaction & Anaphylaxis

  • Distinguish a local allergic reaction from anaphylaxis.
  • Act in the correct order: adrenaline, then call, then position.
  • Understand the role of a prescribed auto-injector without overstepping it.
  • Prepare a household with a known severe allergy.

Allergy vs anaphylaxis

An allergic reaction (hives in one area, itchy eyes after pollen, a local sting welt) stays in its lane. Anaphylaxis is a whole-body reaction — usually involving two or more systems (skin + airway, airway + circulation, GI + circulation) — and can progress to collapse in minutes. Triggers: food (peanut, tree nut, shellfish, milk, egg are the common ones), stings, medication, latex, exercise-related, and sometimes no identifiable cause.

!The recognition pattern

Any breathing difficulty or noise, throat tightness or voice change, widespread hives or flushing, abdominal cramps with vomiting, feeling faint, dizzy or “something is terribly wrong” (that gut feeling is often the hypotension announcing itself) — minutes after a known trigger, or at any time — is anaphylaxis until proven otherwise. The breathing and circulation signs are the ones that kill; the hives are just the alarm bell.

The response — order matters

  1. Adrenaline (epinephrine) first — if prescribed and available. If the person has a prescribed adrenaline auto-injector, help them use it: inject into the outer thigh (through clothing is fine), hold per the device's instructions (~10 seconds), remove, massage the spot briefly. If there's no improvement or it worsens within 5–15 minutes, a second dose may be given (many devices come in pairs). You are not “giving medication” in the clinical sense — you are helping a person use their own prescribed emergency device, and current guidance explicitly supports bystander assistance.
  2. Call emergency services — do not delay this for anything. Even after adrenaline works. Tell them “anaphylaxis, adrenaline given at [time]”. A significant proportion of anaphylaxis bifurcates (reacts again) hours later — that's a hospital-observation decision, not a home decision.
  3. Position: lying down with legs raised if possible — anaphylaxis drops blood pressure, and standing to “feel better” can cause sudden collapse. If breathing is severely difficult, they may prefer sitting semi-upright; if they vomit, recovery position. Do not let them stand or walk once symptoms start.
  4. Monitor and repeat: watch breathing and response continuously; second dose per above if deteriorating; if breathing stops, CPR (Ch. 6). Note times: onset, adrenaline dose(s), any change — the hospital needs the timeline.
  5. Antihistamines are an adjunct, never a substitute — if the person has one and can safely swallow, their clinician may have advised it; it does not treat airway or circulation collapse. Adrenaline treats those. Never delay adrenaline for a tablet.

Preparing a household with a known severe allergy

  • Everyone who lives with or cares for the person knows where the auto-injectors are (two of them, in more than one place — home, school, work bag) and has watched a trainer demo.
  • Check expiry dates monthly (injectors expire; a brown-liquid injector is degraded — replace).
  • The person wears/Carries medical identification and carries a written anaphylaxis action plan from their allergist — it tells any bystander the exact dose, site and timing for their device.
  • Schools, workplaces and restaurants get the plan in writing; “I'll just be careful” is not a management strategy for a peanut allergy.
  • Read labels the way the allergist says to read them; cross-contamination (shared fryers, “may contain”) is the real-world trigger list.
✎Action boundary

You may assist with a prescribed auto-injector (help, position, time it, call). You do not diagnose anaphylaxis for a stranger who has no device — for them, your treatment is: call emergency services, position, monitor, CPR if collapse. And you never delay the call because “they seem better” — the call and the hospital observation are the treatment, not the adrenaline alone.

Key takeaways

  • Breathing or circulation involvement after a trigger = anaphylaxis until proven otherwise.
  • Order: prescribed adrenaline (outer thigh) → call emergency services → lie down, legs up → monitor, second dose 5–15 min if needed.
  • Never let someone stand or walk once symptoms start; never substitute antihistamines for adrenaline.
  • Known severe allergy = a written plan, two unexpired injectors, and trained people around the person.
Review questions
  1. Why must anaphylaxis patients go to hospital even after a successful adrenaline injection?
  2. Why “lying down, legs up” and not “sit up and breathe”?
  3. Your friend with a peanut allergy eats a biscuit, then reports throat tightness and hives on his chest. Walk through 3 minutes.

1) Bifascular (two-phase) reactions occur in a meaningful minority of cases, hours later; hospital observation is the only safety net. 2) Anaphylaxis drops vascular tone and blood pressure; standing or sitting drains the brain and can precipitate sudden collapse, while lying with legs raised uses gravity to support circulation. 3) Help him inject his prescribed auto-injector into the outer thigh (time it), call emergency services stating “anaphylaxis, adrenaline at [time]”, lay him down with legs raised, keep him lying, monitor breathing, and prepare a second dose if he worsens within 5–15 minutes.

Part IV · Chapter 18

Poisoning, Overdose & Chemical Exposure

  • Respond to suspected poisoning without making it worse.
  • Know what never to do (the vomiting myth).
  • Handle chemical and inhalation exposure with scene safety first.
  • Recognise carbon monoxide and opioid-overdose patterns.

The universal rule: don't treat, inform

Poisoning first aid is 90% information and scene safety. The hospital or poison centre needs: what (the container, label, plant photo, or your best description), how much, when, the person's age/weight/conditions, and what has happened since. Keep the container with the person. Call your national poison-control service or emergency services early — they are staffed exactly for this call and will give you the correct, substance-specific instruction. (US: 1-800-222-1222; Australia: 13 11 26; see the India appendix and front-matter guide for finding yours.)

!Never do

Never induce vomiting — current guidance has abandoned it: it risks aspirating the substance into the lungs, and with caustics (drain cleaners, batteries) or hydrocarbons (petrol, solvents) it re-injures the oesophagus on the way back up. Never give milk, oil, salt water, egg white or any “home antidote”. Never leave the person alone. Never give food or drink to anyone drowsy, confused or with reduced consciousness (aspiration risk). For skin/eye contact with a caustic: the flush protocol (20 minutes, Ch. 12) — not a neutralising home remedy.

Common scenarios

Child swallowed something

Clean out the mouth only if you can see and easily reach the item (a coin in a coughing child: encourage coughing, urgent assessment to confirm passage). Button batteries and magnet sets are special emergencies — they cause burns and perforation within hours: emergency department, now, with the packaging. Other products: poison centre call now, don't wait for symptoms.

Medication overdose

Any intentional or suspicious ingestion is a medical and emotional emergency: call emergency services (say “possible overdose” — it routes appropriately), stay with the person, keep them in the recovery position if drowsy, and do not judge on the phone — the call saves the day. For a fully alert adult who took a known small excess of a known medicine, the poison centre's live advice is the right first call. Watch consciousness and breathing until help arrives; if breathing stops, CPR.

Inhalation / fumes

Scene safety first (Ch. 2): fresh air, upwind, no entry into enclosed or low-lying spaces without equipment. Move the person to fresh air only if you can do so without entering the contaminated zone, call, and note the substance. Coughing with clear air is monitored; blue lips, drowsiness or breathing difficulty is an emergency.

Food poisoning

Vomiting + diarrhoea starting hours after a meal: usually self-limiting — the treatment is fluids (oral rehydration solution if available), small frequent sips, rest. Escalate to medical care: blood in stool/vomit, high fever, signs of dehydration (very dark urine, dizziness, fast heartbeat, no tears in a child), confusion, or any high-risk person (under 5, over 65, pregnant, immune-suppressed). Ch. 20 covers the dehydration signs in detail.

Carbon monoxide (CO) — the invisible one

CO is odourless, colourless and silent; it comes from faulty gas appliances, heaters, generators and cars in enclosed spaces. The giveaway is pattern: several people in the same building with headache, nausea, drowsiness and confusion that improves when they leave; a gas-stove flame that's yellow instead of blue; no outdoor source for the symptoms.

  1. Get everyone out to fresh air immediately — this is a scene-safety move, and it's safe because the source is inside.
  2. Do not re-enter to “switch things off” unless the main gas isolate is reachable without entering the contaminated room; ventilate only from outside (open doors/windows from the threshold) if safe.
  3. Call emergency services / the gas emergency service — anyone symptomatic gets medical assessment (oxygen may be needed, and it's not always obvious who's affected).
  4. Prevention: working CO detectors (test monthly, replace per expiry), annual appliance servicing, never running generators or cars in attached garages, never “warming up” a car in a closed garage. CO detectors are the cheapest life insurance in the house.

Suspected opioid overdose — the breathing pattern

Recognition: found unresponsive with slow, shallow or stopped breathing, small pinpoint pupils, gurgling, blue/grey lips — often with paraphernalia nearby, but not always (prescription opioids cause this too). Response: call emergency services immediately; start CPR if breathing has stopped; if a naloxone kit is available and you have been trained or the dispatcher instructs you, use it per the product's instructions and local guidance — naloxone temporarily reverses the overdose, and the person can re-sedate as it wears off, so continued monitoring and hospital care are mandatory. Lay naloxone is an awareness topic in most countries: it is a prescribed or community-programme medication, and use follows local rules, the product leaflet and dispatcher instruction — this book teaches recognition and the call, not independent administration.

✕Myth · "Make them vomit / splash cold water / walk them around"

Vomiting risks aspiration of the poison (and of the vomit) in a drowsy person; cold water and stimulation delay the call and do nothing for the toxin. The evidence-based kit is: scene safety, the early poison-centre/emergency call, the recovery position for the drowsy, CPR for the not-breathing, and the container in hand.

Key takeaways

  • No vomiting, no home antidotes, no food or drink for the drowsy — information and a fast call are the treatment.
  • Keep the container/label/photo with the person; report what, how much, when.
  • Button batteries and magnet swallows in children = emergency department, now.
  • CO: multiple symptomatic people in one building = get out, call, don't re-enter.
  • Opioid pattern = unresponsive + bad breathing: call, CPR if needed, naloxone only per training/local guidance/dispatcher.
Review questions
  1. Why did modern guidance abandon induced vomiting as first aid?
  2. Your neighbour calls: “Two of us have headaches and I feel sick; it went away when I went for a walk.” What do you suspect, and what are the next three actions?
  3. A child swallows a button battery 20 minutes ago and “seems fine”. Route?

1) It adds aspiration risk and a second caustic pass through the mouth and oesophagus, delays definitive care, and the old “fresh sample for the lab” rationale no longer applies — the container is the sample. 2) Carbon monoxide: get everyone out to fresh air, call emergency services / the gas emergency line, and arrange assessment for both of you without re-entering until the source is dealt with. 3) Emergency department immediately — “seems fine” is the classic early state; button batteries cause full-thickness oesophageal burns within 2 hours, and management (and imaging) is time-critical.

Heat, Cold & Environmental Emergencies

  • Tell heat exhaustion from heatstroke — and act on the difference.
  • Manage hypothermia and frostbite safely.
  • Respond to lightning, floods and drowning without adding casualties.
  • Recognise dehydration at every age.

Heat: the two-stage failure

Dehydration (all ages): thirst, dark urine, dry mouth, reduced urination, headache, dizziness; in children add sunken eyes, no tears, a sunken soft-spot, and unusual sleepiness. Treatment is fluids, given slowly (sips — a drowsy child who gulps aspirates); the warning signs above becoming “can't keep anything down” or “getting drowsier” = medical care. Older adults dehydrate silently (reduced thirst drive) — regular fluid offers are a caregiving task (Ch. 23).

Cold: hypothermia & frostbite

  • Hypothermia (core temperature falling): the classic hamburger signs — Hunger, Apathy, Behavior change (confusion, slowness), Unsteady (staggering), Reflexes slowing, Getting cold, Errors in judgement (“I'm fine, let's keep going” is hypothermia talking). Shivering → stopping of shivering is a deterioration sign, not a recovery. Response: get out of the elements, remove wet clothes, wrap in dry layers/blankets (insulate from the ground too), warm non-alcoholic drinks if fully alert, gentle handling (rough handling of a severely hypothermic person can trigger a fatal arrhythmia — move gently), and call emergency services for any confusion, unconsciousness, or stopped shivering. No alcohol ever (it dilates skin vessels and dumps core heat).
  • Frostbite (white/waxy, hard or numb skin — nose, ears, fingers, toes): get to warmth, do not rub (frozen tissue shreds), do not rewarm unless you can keep it rewarmed (refreezing is worse), protect the area from pressure, and seek medical care — rewarming is a controlled clinical procedure for severe cases, and infection risk is real. Snow blindness (UV keratitis from snow/glare): darkness, eye shielding, urgent eye care if pain and tearing persist — sunglasses and wraparounds prevent it (Ch. 26 cross-ref).

Weather, water, altitude

Lightning

When thunder roars, go indoors — the rule is real because strikes happen away from the cloud core. Outdoors with no shelter: get below the tallest thing, avoid open fields, ridges, lone trees and metal; crouch low with feet together and head down as a last resort (it reduces, not eliminates, risk). A struck person is safe to touch — lightning doesn't linger — and may be in cardiac arrest: CPR + AED immediately. Hair standing on end or skin tingling = the strike is about to happen; move now.

Floods

Water + electricity + unknown contents: the three rules are don't walk, don't drive, don't touch — 15 cm of moving water knocks an adult down, 30 cm floats a car, and floodwater carries sewage, fuel and debris. Move to higher ground on foot if directed, follow official evacuation orders, and treat any floodwater contact wound with the Ch. 11 protocol plus a medical check (leptospirosis is the classic hidden infection).

Drowning (awareness)

Drowning is quiet — the “flailing and screaming” is a movie invention; real submersion often looks like a vertical, motionless head in the water, mouth at the surface. Response: throw, don't go (rope, life ring, pole) unless you are a trained water rescuer; once out of the water: check response and breathing, call, and start CPR immediately — rescue breaths matter here (it's an oxygen arrest, and trained rescuers give them early; untrained: hands-only + AED is still the first move). Treat for hypothermia even if the person “recovers” — late deterioration (secondary drowning is the old term; post-respiratory-arrest lung injury is the accurate one) is why every submersion with any loss of consciousness gets hospital assessment.

Altitude

Acute mountain sickness above ~2,500–3,000 m: headache, nausea, fatigue, insomnia on ascent. Stop ascending, rest, hydrate, descend if worse; worsening confusion, cough with pink frothy sputum (lung oedema) or worsening breathlessness at rest (cerebral oedema) are emergencies — descend immediately and call for help. Prevention is gradual ascent and knowing your destination's altitude before the trip, not after the headache.

!The environment calls

Heatstroke, severe hypothermia, drowning with any unconsciousness, lightning strike, multi-person flood exposure, and altitude oedema are all emergency-services situations — the first aid you give is the bridge, and the bridge needs a road. The “prompt medical assessment” tier (heat exhaustion, mild dehydration, superficial frostnip) still gets a same-day clinician's eyes, because the two tiers are diagnosed by the trajectory, and the trajectory is yours to watch.

Key takeaways

  • Heat: confusion + >40 °C = heatstroke = call + aggressive cooling. Tired + cramping = exhaustion = cool, rest, sips.
  • Cold: stopped shivering + confusion = severe hypothermia = gentle handling, insulation, call. No alcohol, ever.
  • Frostbite: no rubbing, no refreeze, medical care.
  • Drowning: throw don't go; out of water → call → CPR; every unconscious submersion gets hospital assessment.
  • Lightning victims are safe to touch; treat as possible cardiac arrest.
Review questions
  1. A hiker at 3,200 m has a headache, then starts coughing pink-tinged fluid and can't catch his breath sitting still. Diagnosis suspicion and two actions?
  2. Why is rough handling dangerous in severe hypothermia?
  3. Heat exhaustion vs heatstroke: what single finding flips the call from “rest and sips” to “emergency services”?

1) Suspected high-altitude pulmonary oedema: descend immediately (don't wait for dawn or “see how he feels”) and call for emergency evacuation/help; keep him sitting, oxygen if available, and monitor. 2) The cold-depressed heart is electrically unstable; rough handling, shaking or hot baths can trigger ventricular fibrillation — hence gentle moves, warm (not hot) rewarming, and clinical management. 3) Any neurological change — confusion, agitation, slurring, collapse — which marks the switch from a balance problem to a brain-perfusion problem.

Diabetes & Other Sudden Medical Problems

  • Recognise low blood sugar and respond within the person's own care plan.
  • Know the “never give food or drink to an unconscious person” rule and its reason.
  • Handle asthma attacks, severe pain, persistent vomiting and infection warning signs.
  • Route each pattern to the correct urgency.

Diabetes: the two opposite crises

Type-1 (and some type-2 on medication) diabetes can swing to hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar) or hyperglycaemia (high, potentially with ketones). They look overlapping (both can confuse) but the response is opposite — which is why the person's own diabetes emergency plan, medical ID and glucose meter decide, not your guess.

Unresponsive + suspected low sugar = recovery position, call, and the clinician's glucose route (IV/glucagon), not your bottle.

Asthma & breathing difficulty

  • Mild–moderate attack: sit upright leaning forward, calm them, help them use their prescribed reliever inhaler (spacer if they have one) exactly as their asthma action plan says — typically a few puffs with the slow-breath technique, waiting per plan. If they have a written plan, the plan outranks this paragraph.
  • Emergency RED signs: speech in single words, ribs pulling in, lips/fingertips blue, the reliever isn't working, or they're too breathless to sit up → call emergency services, stay with them in the most comfortable position, give further reliever puffs only as their plan or the dispatcher directs, and if they collapse, CPR.
  • General breathing difficulty (any cause): sit them upright (lying flat makes it worse), loosen clothing, fresh air, calm presence, and use the RED signs above as your call trigger. Sudden one-sided breathlessness + chest pain can be a pulmonary embolism — that's an ambulance, not an observation.

Severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting & diarrhoea

  • Severe abdominal pain is a pattern-recognition task: sudden and maximal (“the worst pain of my life”, possibly with a bulge or a “popped” sensation — possible ruptured aneurysm or hernia), pain in the lower right with fever (appendicitis pattern), pain + positive pregnancy test or missed period (ectopic pattern — emergency), pain radiating to the back with vomiting (pancreas), or pain in an older adult with confusion (anything, escalated). All of these: nothing heavy by mouth, nothing to mask the pain before assessment unless a clinician says so, and emergency or urgent care — analgesics before diagnosis can erase the signs the surgeon needs.
  • Persistent vomiting/diarrhoea: the disease is often the virus; the killer is dehydration. Fluids in small frequent sips (oral rehydration solution is the evidence-based fluid — it's the specific treatment, not a comfort item), continue light food as tolerated, and escalate at the dehydration signs (Ch. 19) or blood in stool/vomit, high fever, or any high-risk person. Antibiotics are a clinician's tool, not a home shelf item.

Severe infection warning signs (any age, any site)

Spreading redness with red streaks · a hot, swollen, very painful area · fever with rigors (shaking chills) · confusion or unusual drowsiness (especially over 65 — infection in older adults often presents as “just not right”) · fast breathing or fast heartbeat at rest · non-blanching rash (press a glass over it; if the redness doesn't fade, that's a medical emergency — think meningococcal pattern and call immediately) · a person who “is getting worse overnight” is a medical event, not a bad night's sleep.

Medication-related emergencies

Blood-thinner + any significant bleed = emergency (Ch. 8). Insulin/other diabetes meds + missed meal or doubled dose = the hypoglycaemia route above. Any new rash, breathing difficulty, or swelling after starting a new medication = stop per clinician advice and seek assessment (possible drug reaction — and if breathing is involved, it's the anaphylaxis route, Ch. 17). Keep an up-to-date medication list in the household (Worksheet 15) — in every emergency in this book, “what are they taking?” is a question someone will ask, and the list is the answer.

Related HERBULANCE volumes: Book 21 — Respiratory Health (asthma fundamentals and the airway this chapter protects), Book 22 — Kidney Health (why dehydration is a kidney event, not just an uncomfortable one), and Book 20 — Immune System Health (reading infection warning signs in context).

Key takeaways

  • Hypoglycaemia in the alert: fast sugar per their plan, recheck in 15 minutes. Unconscious: nothing by mouth, call, recovery position.
  • The person's own diabetes/asthma action plan outranks generic advice — know where it is for the people you live with.
  • Asthma RED signs: single-word speech, rib pulling, blue lips, reliever failing → call.
  • Severe or patterned abdominal pain: nothing by mouth, emergency or urgent care.
  • Dehydration, not the virus, is what persistent vomiting and diarrhoea kill — small sips of rehydration fluid are the treatment.

Part V

Injury Prevention Through Everyday Life

The best first aid is the injury that never happened. This part is the highest-return section in the book: each habit here prevents the emergencies of Parts II–IV, repeatedly, for years.

Part V · Chapter 21

Household Safety

  • Run a room-by-room hazard inspection that actually gets done.
  • Fix the top household killers: falls, fire, burns, poisons.
  • Set up the detection layer: smoke and CO alarms, tested.
  • Keep an exit plan that works in the dark.

The four household killers, in order of frequency

1 · Slips & falls

Loose rugs (remove or double-side-tape the corners), wet floors mopped with a “wet floor” sign, cables routed away from walking lines, non-slip mats in the tub (fixed, not floating), good lighting on every step, night-lights on the bedroom-to-bathroom path, clutter cleared from hallways. Furniture that can tip: anchor tall units and TVs to the wall — tip-overs are a leading cause of serious head injury in homes with children and older adults.

2 · Fire

Kitchen is origin #1: never leave frying unattended, keep towels and boxes off the burners, know how to smother a pan fire (lid or splash blanket, slide it on from the side, switch off the heat — never water on oil). Dryer lint filtered per manual; space heaters 1 m from anything; candles out of reach of sleep and drapes; electrical sockets not daisy-chained; and the detection layer below is non-negotiable.

3 · Burns & scalds

Water heater at ≤~50 °C / 120 °F (scalds happen in seconds at bath temperature), pots turned handles-in, iron and kettle cords out of reach, and the child-proofing chapter (Ch. 22) for households under 12.

4 · Poisons & medications

Everything toxic on a high shelf or in a locked cabinet — cleaners, pesticides, alcohol, meds in original containers (never “pill boxes” that outlive their labels in a child-reachable spot), batteries and magnets locked away, and the original containers kept for the poison-centre call (Ch. 18). Garages get the same standard as kitchens.

The detection layer

  1. Smoke alarms on every level and outside each sleeping area; interlinked if your region's building code offers it; test monthly (the button), replace per the printed expiry (typically 10 years), and have a “battery day” on the household calendar.
  2. CO detectors near sleeping areas and wherever fuel-burning appliances live; same testing discipline. (Ch. 18: the silent-killer pattern.)
  3. Know two exits from every room — literally walk them, in the dark, once. A window that opens counts. A locked balcony door with a key inside the room doesn't.
  4. Fire plan: meeting point outside (a neighbour's post, a street tree), the rule that everyone gets out and stays out, and the practice drill (Worksheet 12). One drill a year is the difference between a plan and a hope.

Electrical, water and equipment

  • Electrical: no frayed cords (replace, don't tape), no sockets under sinks or in bathrooms without the local protection rating, appliances unplugged (at the plug) before cleaning, and the rule from Ch. 2 — water and electricity incidents are scene-safety incidents first.
  • Water: know where the main water shut-off is (mark it), a bucket in the toilet cistern is the emergency flush, and leak sensors under the washing machine are cheap insurance for the floor below.
  • Equipment: ladders on level ground, three points of contact, no top step as a platform; stairs get a handrail on at least one side (two if the budget allows) and a clean, uncluttered nosing; and anything that lifts people (stairs, attics, lofts) gets the lighting and grip upgrade before the injury, not after.
✓The 20-minute monthly sweep

Worksheet 6 turns this chapter into a checklist. Do it monthly for 10 minutes (rugs, cables, lighting, clutter) and seasonally for 20 (alarms, water heater temperature, heating, dryer filter, outdoor lighting). The households that prevent injuries aren't the careful ones — they're the scheduled ones.

Key takeaways

  • Falls, fire, burns, poisons are the Big Four — each has a 20-minute fix list, not a renovation.
  • Smoke + CO alarms, tested monthly, are the detection layer that works while you sleep.
  • Two exits per room, walked in the dark, once a year — with a named outdoor meeting point.
  • Anchor tall furniture; turn pot handles in; lock the poisons; know the water shut-off.
Review questions
  1. Oil is flaming in a pan and you're the only one in the kitchen. Three actions, in order?
  2. Why do tip-over anchorings matter for older adults too, not just children's homes?
  3. What makes a “fire plan” a plan rather than a hope?

1) Switch off the heat, slide a lid or metal tray over the pan from the side (or a splash blanket), leave it on and off until fully cool — and if it's beyond the lid's control, get out and call fire services from outside. Never water, never carry the pan, never grab the towel. 2) Furniture tip-overs injure anyone who pulls, leans or bumps a tall unanchored unit — the mechanism doesn't check age, and older adults recover from head injuries far less well. 3) The parts you can fail: a named meeting point, a second exit per room, and a practiced drill — plans that haven't been walked in the dark fail in the dark.

Part V · Chapter 22

Child & Infant Safety

  • Apply current safe-sleep guidance correctly.
  • Prevent the classic child-hazard set: choking, burns, poisons, falls, water.
  • Use restraints correctly and know the toy-small-parts rule.
  • Plan age-appropriate emergency readiness with the child.

Safe sleep (the highest-impact single rule)

Current paediatric guidance (AAP and equivalents) is consistent on the core: back to sleep, every sleep, on a firm flat surface in a safety crib, in the same room as the baby but on a separate surface, for at least the first 12 months. No pillows, duvets, blankets, bumpers, stuffed animals or loose clothing in the sleep space; no couches, armchairs or adult beds for unsupervised sleep (the surface where most infant sleep deaths occur); keep the sleep area smoke-free and at a normal room temperature; and breastfeeding, where the family chooses it, is associated with reduced risk. Regional practice varies on specifics (e.g., some regions address swaddling rules separately) — follow your national paediatric body's current statement, and note that co-bedding advice differs between countries, which is exactly why “your national guideline” is the anchor, not a viral video.

The classic hazard set

Choking (under 5)

The food list from Ch. 7 is the prevention list: no whole nuts, popcorn, hard candy, grapes halved-lengthwise or quartered, cherry tomatoes cut, raw carrot sticks for the young; no balloons (any age, honestly); small parts, coins and button batteries off the floor and off low surfaces; supervise every meal; and the CPR + choking course for both parents/caregivers is the single best-purchased safety item in this chapter.

Burns & scalds

Water heater ≤~50 °C / 120 °F; test bath water with a thermometer or elbow before the child goes in; kitchen is a no-walk zone (turn handles in, hot drinks away from table edges, cords pulled behind); iron, kettle and curling tongs on their highest shelf; and “never leave a child in the bath, even for a second” is not a slogan, it's the statistic.

Poisons & ingestion

High or locked cabinets for cleaners, pesticides, alcohol, meds (original containers), and the small-and-shiny set (batteries, magnets, magnets are a paired emergency — Ch. 18); plants reviewed against a local toxicity list (the lily-of-the-valley and oleander in the garden are the classics); and the poison-control number on the kitchen wall, not in a notebook.

Falls

Window guards (with the removable-key type, correctly fitted) on every floor above ground, furniture anchored (Ch. 21), gates at both ends of stairs (top of stairs is the higher-risk one — the gate at the top needs the self-latching type), and no cribs or beds near windows. A child at the window-sill height is a child at the window.

Water

Drowning is the leading cause of childhood injury death in many countries, and it happens in buckets, bathtubs, ponds and pools — not just swimming pools. The rules: never leave a child unattended in or near water (the “reach, throw, don't go” from Ch. 19 applies to rescues); pools get a self-closing, self-latching barrier on all four sides (the fence is the barrier, the alarm is a bonus); buckets get drained and stored upside down; and swim lessons help, but supervision is the control — lessons reduce risk, they don't replace eyes.

Car seats & restraints

Rear-facing as long as the seat's limits allow (current guidance extends rear-facing well beyond the old “outgrow it at 2” rule), then a forward-facing harness seat, then a booster until the seatbelt fits properly (lap belt low on the hips, shoulder belt across the chest — not the neck), and no back-seat exceptions for under 12 in most regions. The car-seat check (fit, expiry, recall) at every vehicle service, and the rule that a child never rides in a car without the correct restraint, ever, even “just to the shop”.

Playground, toys & the supervision layer

  • Toys: the age rating is a small-parts declaration, not a suggestion — under 3 means no small parts, full stop; check for mouth-size hazards (the classic test: anything that fits through a toilet-paper tube is a choking object); and recall checks take 30 seconds on the national product-safety site.
  • Playgrounds: surface under equipment (wood fibre, rubber, sand — not bare concrete or asphalt), equipment gaps checked for head entrapment, and the age-zoned areas respected (the toddler zone and the 8-year-old zone are different risk classes).
  • Supervision is a system, not a virtue: the “water watch” (one named adult, phone down, eyes on the water, rotating on the clock), the “kitchen lockout” (the child's zone while the cooking is live), and the “two-adult rule” for any risk activity (climbing, water, fire, traffic). Boredom and phones are the two supervision killers — the plan beats the intention.

Age-appropriate emergency readiness

Children learn readiness by doing, at their level: “stop, drop, roll” and the fire drill at the meeting point (age 4+), their own name, the parents' names, and the emergency number practised on a pretend phone (age 5+), the “two exits from the bedroom” walk (age 6+), and the stranger/“I got separated” rule with a named safe adult (age 7+). Keep it calm, keep it repeated, and never use the emergency number as a threat or a game — the child who's drilled once a year and rehearsed the number twice a week is the child who calls when it counts. Ch. 28 builds the family plan these skills plug into.

✎Regional differences matter here

Safe-sleep specifics, car-seat law, pool-barrier codes and product-recall systems differ between countries — and within them, between states. This chapter gives the consistent international core; the exact rule is your national paediatric and building-safety body's current statement, and that's the one to print and stick on the fridge.

Related HERBULANCE volumes: Book 12 — Sleep & Circadian Rhythm (the science under the safe-sleep rule) and Book 16 — Skin, Hair & Nails (the burn-healing side of the burns this chapter prevents).

Key takeaways

  • Safe sleep: back, firm, separate surface, same room, nothing loose — and the couch is the danger surface.
  • The small-parts rule (under 3), the water rule (supervision is the control), and the car-seat rule (rear-facing as long as the seat allows) are the three highest-leverage child-safety decisions.
  • Supervision is a system: named watcher, phone down, rotating, two adults for risk activity.
  • Children learn readiness by doing it — fire drill, the number, the two exits — calmly, repeatedly, never as a threat.
Review questions
  1. Why is the couch the specific danger surface for infant sleep, beyond “it's not a crib”?
  2. What makes the pool fence the control and the pool alarm the bonus?
  3. Why “rear-facing as long as the seat allows” rather than “until age 2”?

1) The combination: an inclined/reclined surface that lets the head flop forward (airway occlusion) plus soft bedding and gaps (entrapment and suffocation) — the mechanism is positional asphyxia, and the couch provides both the position and the soft materials in a way a firm flat crib doesn't. 2) Because the fence is the primary barrier that stops the child reaching the water at all, while the alarm only detects a breach that's already happened — a detection layer is only as good as the prevention layer it backs up, and the child-drowning window is seconds, not the alarm's delay. 3) Rear-facing distributes a crash's force across the whole back of the head, neck and spine — the infant's proportionally large head and weak neck make the frontal (forward-facing) position the high-injury one, and modern seats extend rear-facing limits well past the old weight cutoff, so the seat's rating, not the birthday, sets the endpoint.

Older-Adult Safety & Fall Prevention

  • Recognise that a fall in later life is an event, not a misfortune.
  • Run the home-modification list that prevents most falls.
  • Handle the medication, vision, footwear and bone-health contributors.
  • Set up emergency communication and caregiver readiness.

The reframe: falls are the leading preventable injury event in later life

A fall that's “just a fall” under 40 becomes, over 65, the mechanism for hip fracture, head injury and the loss of independent living. The same home that was safe at 35 is a hazard field at 70 — not because the person is “getting old”, but because the risk factors (balance, reaction time, bone density, vision, medication load) have shifted and the home hasn't. The good news: most falls are preventable, and the prevention is a checklist, not a renovation.

The home modification list

  1. Lighting: every step, every path, night-time included — brighter bulbs, a light on the bedroom-to-bathroom route, and the “can you see the edge of every step” test done in the evening light, not at noon.
  2. The bathroom: grab bars by the toilet and in the tub/shower (fitted into the wall studs, not adhesive-only for a full bath), a non-slip mat that's fixed, a shower seat, and the bath replaced by a walk-in shower where the budget and the risk allow.
  3. The paths: rugs removed or fixed, cables routed, clutter cleared, and the “walk it barefoot in the dark” test on every daily path.
  4. The stairs: handrail on both sides, each step's edge clearly visible (a contrasting strip on the nosing), and a chair-rest landing on long flights.
  5. The furniture: armrests on every seat the person uses for standing up (a bed or sofa that's too low makes every stand a fall risk), the firm mattress over the soft one, and the phone and glasses within reach of the bed (the 3 a.m. trip is the classic fall).

The body-side contributors (discuss with the clinician, don't self-manage)

  • Medication review: the “four Ms” — medications that cause dizziness, drowsiness, low blood pressure or confusion (sedatives, some blood-pressure and diabetes drugs, others) are the highest-yield fall-prevention conversation a clinician can have. The annual “is this still needed, and is the dose still right” review is the intervention, not stopping anything independently.
  • Vision & hearing: the current prescription (the last eye test was two years ago and the glasses are on the hook, not on the face, is the finding), cataracts addressed, and hearing checked — balance uses both, and the “I just don't hear the bus” fall is a hearing fall.
  • Footwear: non-slip soles, no loose slippers as indoor footwear (the slipper-is-the-hazard rule), and shoes that lace or secure rather than slide off.
  • Bone health: the fall-prevention and bone-density conversation are the same conversation — a osteoporotic hip turns a survivable fall into a life-changing one, and the screening and treatment (per the clinician) are the bone side of this chapter. Book 24 carries the full bone-health picture.
  • Strength & balance: targeted exercise (the evidence-based fall-prevention programmes — tai chi, balance and strength work — are the intervention, and they're the most under-prescribed medicine in older-adult care). Book 14 has the activity side.

Emergency communication & caregiver readiness

Always reachable

A phone that's actually answered (the big-button and the simple-model exist for a reason), the emergency number and the family's numbers as speed-dials, a medical-ID bracelet or the information card from Worksheet 15 in the wallet, and the “where I am” habit — the person who can tell the dispatcher their address at 3 a.m. is the person who gets help at 3 a.m.

Check-in system

The daily contact (the call, the visit, the “are you okay” text that gets answered) is the detection layer for the falls and the “just not right” days that don't call for help. The missed check-in is the alarm — and it only works if someone's job is to notice the miss.

After a fall

The Ch. 14 red flags apply with a lower threshold: any head knock, any blood-thinner use, any “I can't get up” (the long lie is where the rhabdomyolysis and the hypothermia start — call for help rather than attempting a self-recovery from the floor), and any fall that's a change (a person who doesn't fall, falling, is a medical event to be assessed, not a bad day).

✓The conversation that prevents the worst fall

“Let's look at the medicine list together with the doctor, and check the eyes and the hearing” is the sentence that prevents more older-adult falls than any grab bar. The home mods are the floor; the clinical review is the ceiling; both, together, are the intervention.

Related HERBULANCE volumes: Book 15 — Healthy Ageing & Longevity (the decade-scale picture this chapter protects), Book 24 — Bone & Joint Health (the hip side of the fall), and Book 14 — Physical Activity (the strength and balance prescription).

Key takeaways

  • A fall over 65 is a preventable injury event and a medical signal — not a misfortune and not “just age”.
  • The home mods: light every path, secure the bathroom, clear the paths, rail both sides of the stairs, firm seating with armrests.
  • The clinical review — medication, vision, hearing, bone density, strength — is the other half of the intervention, and it's the higher-yield half.
  • Communication is safety: speed-dials, the card in the wallet, and a check-in someone's job is to notice.
Review questions
  1. Why is the 3 a.m. bedroom-to-bathroom trip the specific high-risk path?
  2. What makes a “long lie” (unable to get up after a fall) an emergency in its own right?
  3. Why is the medication review the highest-yield fall-prevention conversation?

1) The combination of low light, the path that's not lit for it, the bathroom floor that's the most slip-prone surface in the house, and the person who's groggy and possibly on a blood-pressure medication at its peak — the risk factors stack on exactly that route, which is why it gets the night-light and the cleared path first. 2) Because the dangers start at minute zero of the lie: progressive heat loss (hypothermia, especially in cold rooms), muscle pressure (rhabdomyolysis and kidney strain from the sustained position), and the unseen injury (a hip or a head bleed) that the person can't assess from the floor — so the call for help, not the self-recovery attempt, is the correct first move. 3) Because the medication class that causes dizziness, drowsiness and postural low blood pressure is the single largest modifiable fall-risk factor, and it's fully reversible at the annual review — the home mods reduce the consequence, the review removes the cause.

Road, Vehicle & Travel Safety

  • Apply the roadside safety sequence after a collision.
  • Get the restraint and helmet basics right for every mode.
  • Prepare for travel: the vehicle kit, the remote-plan, the contacts.
  • Handle pedestrian, cycle and motorbike risk honestly.

After a collision — the roadside sequence

  1. Stop and assess the scene (Ch. 2): traffic, fuel, fire, electricity. If the vehicle is on a live carriageway and it's drivable and safe, the decision is often to move to the shoulder; if it's not moving or the road is live, everyone gets out and away from the traffic path (behind a barrier, up an embankment — away from the road, not in it) once it's safe to open the doors.
  2. Make the scene visible: hazards on, the warning triangle at the correct distance (local law sets it — typically 45 m on roads, 100 m on motorways, more at night or on curves), reflective vests on if you have them, and lights on in the dark.
  3. Call: emergency services for any injury, any fire, any “I'm not sure”, any spinal-injury mechanism, or any road that can't be made safe. The Ch. 5 information set applies: position (direction, marker, landmark), vehicles, people, injuries, hazards.
  4. First aid in scope: bleeding (Ch. 8), unresponsive (Ch. 6), and the rule that people with neck pain or limb weakness after a collision are treated as spinal-injury suspects until EMS says otherwise (Ch. 14) — you support, you don't move them.
  5. Document, then hand over: the observations and times you'll give the responders and (later) the insurers; the photos only once the scene is safe and you're not the last person at it. The hospital and the responders come before the paperwork, always.

Travel preparation

  • The vehicle kit (Worksheet 10): the Ch. 4 vehicle list plus the warning triangle, reflective vest, torch, power bank, water, the first-aid supplies, and the local emergency number written down (the phone dies, the paper doesn't).
  • Remote & long trips: the check-in plan told to someone before departure (the route, the stops, the expected arrival — the missed check-in is the alarm, Ch. 5); the satellite messenger or the offline maps for the no-signal stretches; the fuel rule (never below half on the remote stretch); and the “where's the next town with a hospital” check on the route, not at the breakdown.
  • International travel: the local emergency and poison numbers before departure (front-matter guide), the insurance's 24/7 line, the medication in the carry-on with the documentation (the prescription in the original language plus the translation), the destination's snakebite/marine-sting card (Ch. 16), and the “which hospital, which way” saved in the phone notes for the base.
  • Commercial travel (air, rail, long-haul bus): the aisle-seat awareness (you can't help from the window seat), the “I'm the first-aider on this flight” reality (the tannoy will find you — be the person who steps forward, and know your limits), and the travel first-aid checklist (Worksheet 11) in the carry-on, not the checked bag.
!The motorway rules that prevent the second crash

On a hard shoulder or motorway emergency stop: hazards on, everyone out and over the barrier (not standing by the vehicle, not in the lane, not “just checking the tyre” on the carriageway), the triangle only if it can be placed from the safe side. The second crash — the one that hits the stopped vehicle or the people around it — is where the roadside deaths happen, and it's prevented entirely by getting off the road and staying off it.

Key takeaways

  • Collision sequence: safe scene → visible (triangle, vest, lights) → call → in-scope first aid → document, hand over. People with neck pain or limb weakness are spinal suspects until EMS says otherwise.
  • The restraint set: belt every seat every trip, the correct child restraint, the helmet that's certified and under the strap — and the “just to the shop” exception is the exception that kills.
  • Remote travel runs on the check-in plan and the fuel rule; the missed check-in is the alarm that works.
  • The second crash is preventable: out, over the barrier, stay off the road.
Review questions
  1. Two cars have collided on a live two-lane road; both drivers are out and one reports neck pain. Your sequence?
  2. Why is the back seat the higher-injury seat in a frontal crash, and what does that change about the belt rule?
  3. What is the “second crash”, and what single behaviour prevents it on a motorway?

1) Make the scene visible (hazards, triangle from the safe side), call emergency services with the position and the “one driver reporting neck pain”, keep the neck-pain driver still and supported (Ch. 14 bystander role), check the other for bleeding and response, keep traffic back, and hand over the timeline — no moving of either driver by you. 2) Because a frontal crash loads the front-seat occupants against the belt and airbag while the unrestrained or mis-belted back-seat occupant becomes a projectile into the front seats — so the belt rule is “every seat, every trip”, with the back seat carrying the equal obligation, not the exception. 3) The collision with the stopped vehicle or the people around it by following traffic; getting out, over the barrier, and staying off the carriageway (with the triangle only from the safe side) is the single behaviour that removes the people from its path.

Part V · Chapter 25

Workplace, School & Recreational Safety

  • Know the workplace hazard layers and where you sit in them.
  • Set up the office and the school incident basics.
  • Prepare for sport, outdoors and water recreation with the right kit and the right plan.
  • Use the reporting and emergency-action-plan loop.

Workplace: the layers

Office

The ergonomic basics (screen at eye level, feet flat, the chair that's adjusted for you not the last person), the cable and the walkway (Ch. 21's home rules apply to the office), and the fire plan that's actually drilled — the evacuation route you've never walked is the one you'll walk in the smoke. The workplace first-aid kit (the larger Ch. 4 variant) and the named trained first-aider on the roster are the expectation in most jurisdictions, and asking “who's the first-aider, where's the kit, where's the AED” on day one is the correct new-start question.

Machinery & the manual

The guards on, the lockout understood (the machine that's being repaired is the machine that must not start — the lockout/tagout procedure is the control, and it's not a suggestion), the PPE that's fitted for the task (the gloves for the cut, the ear protection for the noise, the eye protection for the spark — the PPE is the last layer, after the engineering control and the procedure, not the first), and the “I'll just quickly” rule broken: the quick unguarded adjustment is the amputation statistic.

The reporting loop

The near-miss reported is the accident prevented: the reporting channel that's actually used (not the one that punishes the reporter), the hazard that's logged and closed, and the emergency-action plan that's known (the assembly point, the muster, the roles) and rehearsed. A workplace's safety is measured by the near-misses it reports, not the accidents it doesn't have — the absence of reported near-misses is the warning sign, not the success sign.

School: the incident basics

  • The rostered trained responder: the school's first-aid provision (the trained staff, the kit, the AED where required by local law) is the structure; the individual teacher's role is the Ch. 2 sequence — scene safe, call for the trained responder and emergency services as indicated, in-scope care, and the parent-notification per the school's policy (the notification is a procedure, not a judgement).
  • The common school incidents: the nosebleed (Ch. 8, sit forward, 10 minutes), the playground fall (the Ch. 13/14 recognition — the head knock gets the red-flag watch), the swallowed small object (Ch. 7/18), the bee sting (Ch. 16, with the anaphylaxis watch — the school's prescribed-injector protocol for the child with the known allergy), and the “I'm not feeling well” that's actually the early infection or the hypoglycaemia (Ch. 20) — the watch-and-report is the teacher's first aid.
  • The assembly and the drill: the fire and the lockdown drills, done on the schedule, are the rehearsal that makes the real event a procedure rather than a panic — and the children who've drilled are the children who move calmly, which is the safety.

Sport, outdoors & water recreation

Sport & contact

The pre-participation check (the hydration, the warm-up that's the injury prevention, the equipment that fits), the concussion protocol (Ch. 14 — the “sit out until cleared” rule that the peer pressure fights), and the first-aid provision that's proportionate to the contact (the collision sport carries more than the court sport, and the kit reflects it). The “play through the pain” culture is the injury culture — the report and the rest are the discipline.

Hiking & camping

The tell-someone plan (the route, the return time, the missed-check-in alarm — Ch. 5), the outdoor kit (the Ch. 4 outdoor variant plus the navigation, the water treatment, the insulation for the weather that's not on the forecast), the turn-around time decided before the summit (the “we're close” that's the accident), and the altitude awareness (Ch. 19) for the high routes. The group rule: the slowest member sets the pace, and the group turns back together or not at all.

Water recreation

The swim-ability honest assessment (the “I can swim” that's the pool-ability, not the open-water ability), the buddy system and the visible watcher for the children (Ch. 22's water rule applies to the beach and the lake as much as the pool), the conditions check (the rip-current knowledge for the surf, the cold-shock awareness for the cold water — the gasp-and-sink of the cold immersion is why the “swim, float, call” is the river and cold-water rule), and the boat basics (the life jacket on, the VHF or the phone's waterproofing, the “where's the nearest shore” known before the launch).

Community events

The large-gathering reality: the crowd density awareness (the personal-space-that's-being-lost is the signal to move to the edge or out), the meeting point agreed before the event for the children and the group (the “if we're separated, we meet at X” that's the reassembly plan), the water and the shade for the heat (Ch. 19's rules apply to the crowd as much as the individual), and the “where's the first-aid post, where's the exit” walked at the entry, not needed at the emergency. The event's own emergency plan exists — finding it at the entry is the participant's part.

✓The AED question to ask at every workplace and venue

“Where's the AED, and who's trained to use it?” is the single highest-yield safety question for a building with more than a handful of people — the public-access defibrillation coverage (Ch. 6) is the difference between the collapse that's a tragedy and the collapse that's a Tuesday afternoon with a scar. If the answer is “we don't have one”, that's the gap to raise with the management, and the national bodies' AED-registry maps show what's already nearby.

Key takeaways

  • Workplace safety runs on layers: engineering control, procedure, PPE, and the reporting loop that closes the near-miss — the unreported near-miss is the warning.
  • School first aid is the Ch. 2 sequence plus the roster: scene safe, call the trained responder, in-scope care, notify per policy.
  • Outdoors runs on the tell-someone plan, the turn-around time set before the summit, and the group-moves-together rule.
  • Water recreation runs on the honest swim assessment, the visible watcher, and the conditions check before the entry.
  • “Where's the AED, and who's trained?” is the question for every building you spend time in.
Review questions
  1. Why is the absence of reported near-misses a warning sign rather than a success sign?
  2. What is the “turn-around time”, and why is it set before the summit?
  3. What makes the cold-shock response the reason for the “swim, float, call” river rule?

1) Because a safe workplace generates near-misses all the time and reports them — the reporting is the detection system; zero reports means either zero risk (rare) or a reporting culture that isn't working, and the unreported near-miss is the accident that hasn't had its bad day yet. 2) The time by which the group commits to turning back, set at the start against the fitness, the weather and the light — because the decision made at the summit, with the summit in view and the group tired, is the decision that's overridden by the “we're close”, and the pre-set time removes that moment from the negotiation. 3) Because the cold immersion triggers an involuntary gasp and a hyperventilation that can cause immediate water ingestion and a sink before the swimmer can act — so the rule is not to fight the current or swim to shore in the shock, but to calm down, float, and call for help or signal, which is survivable where the panicked swim often isn't.

Building a Prevention-First Lifestyle

  • Turn the checklists into a standing rhythm, not a one-off project.
  • Teach the family the emergency language and the roles.
  • Keep the skills current with the refresher cycle.
  • Build the community awareness that multiplies your readiness.

From project to rhythm

Preparedness that happens once — the big kit-buy, the one-off home inspection — decays. Preparedness that's a rhythm compounds. The rhythm this book asks for is deliberately small: the monthly 10-minute kit and alarm check (Ch. 4, Ch. 21), the seasonal 20-minute sweep, the yearly drill and the yearly clinical conversation (the fall-prevention review, Ch. 23), and the skill refresher on the training body's cycle. None of it is an hour; all of it together is the difference between the household that's ready and the household that's buying ready.

The family emergency language

The words that mean things

Agree the vocabulary in advance: the “meet at the point” (the Ch. 28 meeting point), the “all clear” and the “call now” (the distinction between the check-in and the emergency), and the “I'm at the hospital, room X” text template that the whole family can send without thinking. The words agreed in the calm are the words that work in the event — the family that has to invent the language during the emergency is the family that's spending the minutes on the vocabulary instead of the action.

The roles

Who calls (the primary and the backup), who grabs the go-bag (Ch. 29), who's responsible for the children, who's responsible for the older adult or the pet, and who's the “we're all here” confirmer at the meeting point. The roles are assigned by capability, not by age or rank, and they're drilled — the drill is where the role that's on paper becomes the role that's in the muscle memory.

The information that's findable

The medication list (Worksheet 15), the insurance and the contacts (Worksheet 3), the documents (Ch. 28), and the “where everything is” that the whole household knows — the kit's location, the go-bag's location, the shut-offs, the alarm test date. The information that's in one person's head is the information that's lost in the event; the information that's written, stored and known is the information that works.

The skill refresher cycle

First-aid and CPR skills are physical, and physical skills decay on the training body's timescale — the refresher on the certification cycle (typically 1–2 years, per the training body) is the maintenance, and it's where the guideline updates (the 2025 changes in Ch. 6 and Ch. 7) reach the household. The household that keeps one or two adults current is the household where the “I read about it” becomes the “I've done it” — and the difference, in the moment, is the difference between the hesitation and the action. The course is the purchase; the refresher is the habit.

Community awareness — the multiplier

  • The neighbour network: the know-your-neighbour that's the check-in for the older adult next door (Ch. 23's system, extended), the “who's the first-aider on the street” that the community event or the block can name, and the shared-resource awareness (the AED at the shop, the defibrillator at the community hall) — the community that knows its own first-aid geography is the community where the bystander is the responder.
  • The accessible arrangement: the readiness plan that includes the disability and the access needs (Ch. 28's section) — the evacuation that assumes every member can climb, run and hear the alarm is the plan that fails the member who can't, and the accessible version (the ground-floor safe room, the neighbour's lift, the alert that's audible and visible) is the one that works for everyone, which is the point of “everyone”.
  • The teaching that multiplies: the household that teaches the visiting family, the friend, the new neighbour the five-minute version (the roadmap, Ch. front-matter; the choking and the CPR concepts) is extending the coverage without a course — and the formal course, when the community takes it, is where the coverage becomes the capability. The awareness is the seed; the training is the tree.

Key takeaways

  • Preparedness is a rhythm — the monthly 10 minutes, the seasonal 20, the yearly drill and review — not a one-off project.
  • The family agrees the words and the roles in the calm, and drills them, because the language and the roles invented in the event cost the minutes the action needs.
  • The information that's written and known works; the information that's in one head is lost in the event.
  • The refresher cycle is where the guidelines reach the household and the reading becomes the doing.
  • Community awareness multiplies: the know-your-neighbour check-in, the first-aid geography, and the accessible plan that works for every member.

  2. What does the “accessible version” of the evacuation plan change, concretely?
  3. Why is the community's first-aid geography (the AED at the shop, the trained neighbour) a safety asset?

1) Because a one-off state (the new kit, the inspected home) is a snapshot that the normal wear — the expiry, the battery, the moved furniture, the forgotten role — erodes continuously, while the rhythm re-captures the state on a schedule faster than the erosion, so the readiness is re-established before it's lost rather than recovered after it's gone. 2) It replaces the assumptions: the ground-floor safe room for the member who can't use the stairs, the neighbour's lift or the vehicle arrangement for the member who can't walk the evacuation route, and the alert that's audible and visible (the strobe, the vibration) for the member who can't hear the alarm — the plan that names each member's actual route instead of the average member's route. 3) Because the response time in the first minutes is the bystander's, and the bystander who knows the AED is thirty metres away and who knows which neighbour is trained closes the gap that the ambulance's travel time can't — the community's coverage is the layer of response that exists before the professional layer arrives, and it's free once it's known.

Preparing for Natural Disasters

  • Know which hazards actually apply to your region — and ignore the rest.
  • Understand the warning, the evacuation and the shelter decision.

The first rule: localise or skip

The three decisions every hazard shares

  1. Warning vs evacuation vs shelter: the warning stage is when you finish the preparation (the top-up, the charge, the “we can still leave” window); the evacuation instruction is the leave-now decision — and it's taken at the instruction, not at the visible damage (the “it's only rain” that's the evacuation-refusal statistic); the shelter decision (the in-place protection) is the “we can't or shouldn't move” case, and it has its own per-hazard protocol (the interior room for the cyclone, the drop-cover for the quake, the upper-floor-for-flood where it's safe and directed). The household knows which it will face, because the local list (above) says so.
  2. The official-source discipline: the national disaster agency's channel (the app, the broadcast alert, the official site) is the source; the social feed is the noise. The alert that's official and the post that's viral look similar in the panic, and the household that's subscribed to the official channel in the calm is the household that acts on the true signal in the event. The “is this official” check takes five seconds and prevents the false-evacuation and the missed-evacuation alike.
  3. The communication plan under the outage: the disaster that takes the power takes the charging, and the one that takes the network takes the call — so the plan includes the out-of-band layer: the out-of-area contact (the relative outside the region that everyone calls, because the long-distance route survives the local congestion), the radio (the battery/crank type that's the no-power news), and the “everyone checks in to X by Y” that's the muster without the meeting point. Ch. 28 builds this into the family plan.
!The two refusals that kill

The evacuation refusal (“it's only rain”, “we've lived here forty years”, “we'll leave if we have to”) — the instruction exists because the authority sees what you can't from the kitchen window, and the window closes. And the return-too-soon (“the water's gone, we'll go back and check”) — the return is at the official all-clear, because the gone-water hides the weakened structure, the downed line and the contaminated ground. Both refusals are the calm-voice decisions the household pre-commits to in the plan, so the event isn't the moment to negotiate with them.

Key takeaways

  • Prepare deeply for the hazards your region actually has — the local list is the curriculum, and the rest is background.
  • The three shared decisions: warning (finish prep), evacuation (leave at the instruction), shelter (the in-place protocol) — and the household knows which it faces.
  • The official channel is the signal; the feed is the noise — subscribed in the calm, trusted in the event.
  • The outage layer: the out-of-area contact, the no-power radio, and the check-in time that's the muster without the meeting point.
Review questions
  1. Why is the evacuation taken at the instruction rather than at the visible damage?
  2. What is the “out-of-area contact”, and why does it survive the local outage?
  3. Why is the cyclone's eye the “false calm” rather than the all-clear?

1) Because the instruction is issued from the picture the authority has — the gauge, the model, the upstream station — that the household can't see from the window, and the visible damage in your street lags the arrival; by the time it's visible in your street, the safe-leave window (the open road, the working vehicle, the calm decision) has typically closed, which is exactly why the rule is pre-committed. 2) The one relative or friend outside the affected region that every household member calls to check in — because the local network is congested or down while the long-distance route to the outside region is often still carrying, the outside contact becomes the hub that confirms each member without the local network having to work. 3) Because the eye is the calm centre of the still-spinning system — the wind returns, from the opposite direction, with the force of the first pass plus the debris and the saturation the first pass created, and the household that treats the calm as the all-clear is the household that's outside (or on the roof) when the second, worse, pass arrives.

Part VI · Chapter 28

Creating a Household Emergency Plan

  • Write the plan the family will actually run — contacts, meeting points, routes.
  • Build in the children, the older adults, the pets and the access needs.
  • Handle the power and communication outage as a designed-for case.
  • Drill it — the plan that's been walked is the plan that works.

The plan's spine: the four pages

The whole plan fits on four pages, and that's the point — the plan that's forty pages is the plan that's not read. Worksheets 3, 12, 13 and 14 in the Toolkit are these four pages, printable and localisable:

Page 1 · Contacts

The emergency number (local, verified — front matter), the poison centre, the out-of-area check-in contact (Ch. 27), each member's phone, the neighbours' (the two closest, at minimum), the doctor and the vet, the insurance, and the landlord/property line. The page that's on the fridge and in every member's phone — because the fridge page is the one the child finds, and the phone copy is the one the adult has in the dark.

Page 2 · Meeting points

Two, not one: the nearby one (the street tree, the corner post — the “the house is the problem” point, used for the fire and the immediate-evacuation) and the out-of-neighbourhood one (the relative's, the school, the park across town — the “we can't get home” point, used for the longer evacuation). Both are places every member can get to and find their way to, and both are drilled, because the meeting point the six-year-old can't navigate is the meeting point that doesn't exist.

Page 3 · Routes & shut-offs

The two evacuation routes out of the home and out of the neighbourhood (the primary and the “the primary's closed” alternative), the internal exits per room (Ch. 21's two-exits rule), and the shut-off locations (the gas, the water, the main power) with the tool that operates them (the wrench that's at the gas valve, not in the shed). The route map drawn by the family, with the children's input, is the route map the family will run.

Page 4 · The special-needs page

Per member, the line that's specific: the medication and the dose and the “the backup supply is in X” for the medical member; the device and its power and its backup for the oxygen or the mobility member; the food and the routine for the pet; the communication method for the member who's Deaf or hard of hearing (the visible alert, the text, the neighbour's shake) or who's cognitively different (the picture-card version of the plan, at their level). The page that makes “everyone” actually everyone — Ch. 26's accessible rule, in writing.

The members' clauses

  • Children: the plan at their level (the picture version, the “if I'm at school, the school's plan takes over and I go to X”, the practice of the number and the names — Ch. 22's readiness), and the school's own emergency contact and pickup rules confirmed in writing (the “who's allowed to collect” list that the school holds, kept current — the list that's out of date is the child who waits).
  • Older adults: the Ch. 23 integration — the check-in that's the detection, the ground-floor safe room or the lift-arranged neighbour for the evacuation, the medication's 72-hour buffer (the go-bag's medical section, Ch. 29), and the mobility device's plan (the “the stairs are the problem” that the plan solves before the event, not during it).
  • Pets: the carrier that's practised (the pet that's never been in the carrier is the pet that's in the carrier for the first time in the panic), the food and the record (the vet's details, the vaccine card), and the “the pet-friendly shelter” list for the region — because the standard shelter that doesn't take the pet is the reason the household that has the pet delays or refuses the evacuation, and the pet-friendly list removes that reason in the calm.
  • Disability & access needs: the member-specific line from Page 4, plus the personal emergency evacuation plan that many jurisdictions' fire and disability services will help write (the “what I need to get out, and who helps” that's the formal document the first responders can act on) — the household that's written it, and shared it with the service, is the household the service can actually help.

The outage, designed for

  1. Power out (hours to days): the light that's not the match (the torch, the power bank's lamp — the candle is the fire-statistic, Ch. 21), the food that's the no-cook (the go-bag's and the pantry's shelf-stable, Ch. 29), the communication that's the low-draw (the crank radio, the text-over-the-call because the text carries on the weaker signal), and the cold's management (the layer, the room-together for the heat-share, the CO rule if the alternative heat is the generator — outside, always).
  2. Communication out: the out-of-area contact (Ch. 27) as the hub, the check-in time as the muster, and the “the silence is the signal” agreement — the member who hasn't checked in by the time is the member the others go for or call for, and the rule is pre-agreed so the silence means something instead of meaning nothing.
  3. Water out: the stored water (Ch. 29's quantity), the “the tap's back on” flush-before-use, and the hygiene-minimum that's the infection prevention (the hand-rub, the “clean, then clean, then clean” for the food and the wound) — the water-outage's real risk is the infection that follows, not the thirst.

The drill — the plan becomes the plan

Once a year, the household runs it: the alarm (the agreed sound or the actual smoke-alarm test), the meet at the nearby point, the count, the “what was slow” debrief that's the improvement (the “the go-bag was in the garage” that becomes the “the go-bag is by the door”), and the timing that's the motivation (the “we were out in four minutes” that's the number the family remembers). The drill is thirty minutes a year and it's the difference between the plan on the fridge and the plan in the muscle — the same logic as the Ch. 26 rhythm, applied to the family as the unit.

Key takeaways

  • Four pages: contacts, meeting points (two), routes & shut-offs, and the special-needs line per member — printable on the Toolkit worksheets.
  • “Everyone” means everyone: the child's picture version, the older adult's check-in and safe room, the pet's practised carrier and the pet-friendly shelter list, and the access-needs member's written personal plan.
  • The outage is designed for: the no-match light, the no-cook food, the out-of-area contact as the hub, and the check-in time as the muster.
  • The yearly drill is what turns the document into the capability — thirty minutes, the debrief included.
Review questions
  1. Why two meeting points rather than one?
  2. What does the pet-friendly shelter list remove from the evacuation decision?
  3. Why is the drill's debrief (“what was slow”) the part that improves the plan?

1) Because the two failures they cover are different: the nearby point is for the “we're out of the house but can't go far” case (the immediate fire, the local evacuation) where the out-of-town point is unreachable, and the out-of-neighbourhood point is for the “we can't get home at all” case (the longer evacuation, the “home is the problem” disaster) where the nearby point is inside the affected zone — one point can't serve both geometries. 2) It removes the “we can't leave without them / they can't leave without us” delay — the knowledge that a named shelter will take the pet means the pet is not the reason the household sits through the evacuation window, which is the delay the refusal statistic is made of. 3) Because the plan's flaws are only visible under the run — the go-bag in the wrong room, the route that's actually blocked, the member who's slow at the door — and the debrief captures them while they're fresh and converts them into the specific fix (the moved bag, the second route, the earlier-start rule) that the next drill then verifies; the plan improves by the loop of run-measure-fix, not by the rewrite.

Emergency Supplies & Go-Bag

  • Size the supply to the household, the hazard and the climate — not to a generic list.
  • Build the go-bag that's grab-and-go in under five minutes.

The sizing rule before the list

Every quantity in this chapter scales by household size × hazard duration × climate. The common starting point in official guidance is three days of supplies for the in-place case (the power/water/comms outage, the shelter-in-place), extended to seven for the regions and hazards where the return is slower (the post-cyclone, the post-earthquake infrastructure case) — and the go-bag itself is the per-person, immediately portable subset that works for the “we're moving now” case, where three days in a bag is impossible and the bag carries the essentials that bridge to the shelter or the vehicle. The climate adjusts the mix (the cold region's insulation and heat, the heat region's water and cooling), and the household's medical needs adjust the medical section. The rotation — the supplies that stay alive

  1. The date system: every consumable (the water if it's the bottled, the food, the medication, the batteries if they're the dated type) gets the expiry on the label or the bag's index, and the index is the first page of the bag.
  2. The half-life swap: at each six-month check (the Ch. 4 monthly covers the kit; the six-month covers the bag and the pantry), the expiring item is used in normal life and replaced — the water that's drunk at the family dinner, the food that's the lunch, the battery that's in the torch for the month — so the rotation funds itself from the consumption instead of the waste. The “use it or lose it” is the rotation, and it's why the supplied household's grocery bill doesn't rise: the stock is the pantry, just dated and indexed.
  3. The weight and the grab test: the bag that's the grab-and-go is the bag that's carryable by the member who'll carry it — the test is the actual carry, by the actual member, from the bag's location to the door, with the content full. The bag that fails the test gets the content trimmed; the bag that's by the door (not in the cupboard, not in the garage) is the bag that's the grab-and-go.
▲The quantities are a starting point, not a prescription

The water-per-day, the three-versus-seven days, and the food mix are the common official starting points, and they're adjusted by the household's actual consumption, the region's climate and water reliability, the hazard's expected duration from the local agency, and the members' needs — the infant's and the medication member's requirements override the generic quantity, and the local emergency guidance (which the Worksheet 16 local list points to) is the authority for the region's specific numbers. This chapter gives the logic and the common starting points; the local guidance and the household's reality set the final figures.

Community Response & Psychological First Aid

  • Help the community without becoming the second casualty or the bottleneck.
  • Apply the psychological first aid principles — and know where they end.
  • Support children and the vulnerable through and after the event.

The community's first hour

The gap between the event and the professional arrival is the community's, and the community that's prepared for that gap helps; the community that's not adds to the load. The principles:

  • Don't become the casualty or the bottleneck: the Ch. 2 scene-safety rule applies to the volunteer as much as the bystander (the well-meaning entry into the smoke, the walk into the floodwater, the crossing of the live traffic to “help” are the community's preventable losses), and the information bottleneck is the quieter one — the person at the scene who's genuinely useful is the person who sees and reports (the “there's an unresponsive man at X, I'm with him” that frees the responder to go to the next) rather than the person who's consumed by one case and can't see the street. The watch-and-report role is a first-aid role.
  • The basic coordination: the informal “who's doing what” that the first minutes need — the person who's called, the person who's with the injured, the person who's meeting the ambulance at the road (the “the crew can't find the house” is the community's recurring failure, and the meet-and-guide volunteer fixes it), the person who's clearing the doorway and the path. The names aren't the rank; the names are the roles, and the roles are the Ch. 28 family plan extended to the block.
  • Supporting the vulnerable first-visible: the older adult who's slow to the meeting point, the member with the access need, the child who's separated — the Ch. 23/26/28 clauses, applied by the neighbour who knows they exist. The community that's mapped its vulnerable members (the check-in network, the “who needs the lift”) is the community the responders can clear fastest, because the community tells them where the help is needed before the sweep finds it.
  • Communicating with the responders: the handover that's the Ch. 5 information set at the street scale (what happened, where, how many, what's been done, what's still unsafe), and the restraint that's part of it — the crowd that's kept back from the scene is the responders' working space, and the neighbour who holds the line at the safe distance is doing the first aid the crowd doesn't know its name.

Psychological first aid (PFA) — the principles, and the boundary

PFA is the humane, practical support in the immediate hours and days after the event — it is not counselling, not therapy, not the processing of the trauma (that's the trained clinician's work, weeks to months later), and this book does not teach it as such. The principles, from the international PFA guidance (the WHO/UNHCR framework and the national adaptations), are the look-listen-support structure:

LookObserve the physical and the emotional safety, and the immediate needsThe “are you hurt, are you warm, have you eaten, is your family accounted for” — the basic needs first, because the unmet physical need is the dominant distress, and meeting it is the first calming. ✎The PFA boundary, stated plainly

You are not the counsellor, and the event is not the session. PFA is the safe presence, the basic needs, the practical connection, and the referral — it does not extract the trauma narrative, it does not diagnose (“you have PTSD” is the phrase that's the harm, from the lay mouth), it does not promise (“you'll be over this by weekend” is the minimisation), and it does not continue past the immediate period into the processing that's the clinician's. The volunteer who knows this boundary is the volunteer who helps; the volunteer who doesn't is the well-meaning source of the secondary harm, and the boundary is the protection for both.

Supporting children through and after

  • The immediate: the safety-and-togetherness first (the child's distress is the separation-and-unknown, and the “you're safe, I'm here, here's what's going to happen next in the words that's true” is the response — the small true next-step beats the big reassurance, because the child's world is the next-step scale), the basic needs (the water, the wrap, the comfort-item from the Ch. 29 bag), and the adult calm as the regulation — the child reads the adult's face as the truth about the danger, and the adult who's visibly composed is the regulation the child can't generate, which is why the PFA's “look-listen-support” runs through the adult's own state first.
  • The language: the words that's the event at the child's level, without the false-comfort and without the graphic-detail — the “there was a fire and the fire service is here and you're safe” rather than the “nothing happened” (the lie the child will detect and that teaches the child that the truth is unsafe to hear) and rather than the unasked detail (the child who's not asked doesn't need the picture, and the one who's asked will ask at their own pace).
  • The after-days: the return-to-routine as the recovery (the bed, the school, the meal-time — the routine is the safety-signal the child's nervous system reads, and the disrupted routine is the continuing alarm), the expected-reactions naming (the bed-wetting's return, the cling, the anger, the replay-play are the child-processing, and the “this is how the body handles it, and it passes” said to the parent is what keeps the parent from reading the processing as the damage), and the watch-line (the reaction that's persistent and worsening over the weeks, or that's disabling the school and the sleep, is the “the professional's turn” signal — the child-and-adolescent mental-health route, per the region's service).

The community recovery & the volunteer's boundary

The recovery layer is the weeks-to-months: the mutual-aid that's the practical (the meal, the lift, the “I'm taking Tuesday”), the community space that's the togetherness (the gathering that's the normal-togetherness restored, which is the social fabric the event cut), and the secondary-victim awareness that's the volunteer's own care — the responder and the volunteer who's carried the load of the others' distress needs the same PFA the principles describe (the peer-debrief that's the structured version, the “who's looking after the lookers” that the organised response builds in), and the volunteer organisation's role is to provide it, because the community that burns out its helpers in the first month is the community that's without them for the second. The boundary, restated: the community helps, coordinates, supports, refers, and cares for its own — and it does all of it inside the scene-safety and competence rules every other chapter in this book has been building.

Related HERBULANCE volume: Book 12 — Sleep & Restorative Living is the recovery-side reference for the sleep-disruption that follows the event (the adult and the child), and Book 19 — Brain Health for the stress-response physiology the PFA's calm is working with.

Key takeaways

  • The community's first hour runs on the scene-safety rule, the watch-and-report role, the meet-and-guide for the responders, and the vulnerable-maps that tell the crew where the help is.
  • PFA is look-listen-support: the basic needs, the present listening without the probe or the minimise, the practical connection, and the referral — not counselling, and the boundary is the skill.
  • Children are regulated by the adult's calm, answered with the small true next-step, and recovered through the routine — with the watch-line for the persistent and the worsening.
  • The community that cares for its own helpers (the secondary-victim layer) is the community that has helpers for the long recovery, not just the first hour.
Review questions
  1. Why is “nothing happened” the wrong thing to say to the child, in both directions (the lie and the unasked detail)?
  2. What is the PFA referral, and why is it called the skill rather than the failure?
  3. What is the “secondary victim”, and what does the organised response build in for them?

1) Because the false-comfort (“nothing happened”) is detected by the child as the untruth-in-a-safe-adult, which teaches the child that the truth about the danger is something the adult can't say, and so the child keeps the alarm private where it can't be soothed; and the unasked graphic detail is the re-living imposed before the child is ready, which is the intrusion the processing wasn't asking for — the middle path is the true, small, next-step answer, given at the child's pace, which keeps the truth safe to hear and the detail out until it's asked for. 2) The handover of the person whose reaction is beyond the PFA's reach — the persistent deterioration, the self-harm talk, the disabling continuation — to the trained mental-health professional; it's the skill because the recognition of the boundary (the “this is now the clinician's, and here's the route”) is the hardest part of the PFA, and the volunteer who can do it has done the PFA completely, whereas the volunteer who can't has either overstepped (the harm) or under-served (the missed need) — the referral is the completion, not the abdication. 3) The responder and the volunteer who, in carrying the others' distress and exposure to the event, develops the same stress reactions the event caused in the community — the intrusion, the exhaustion, the withdrawal that's the secondary-victim load; and the organised response builds in the peer support and the structured debrief and the “who's looking after the lookers” rotation, because the helper's unrecovered reaction is the community losing its capacity in the second month, which is when the long recovery's need for them peaks.

My Personal Emergency Readiness Plan

  • Run the personal risk assessment that sizes everything else.
  • Consolidate the book's checklists into one annual review.
  • Set the readiness goals that are the next twelve months.
  • Close the loop: the plan that's reviewed is the plan that's real.

How to use this chapter

This is the working chapter — the one that's done with a pen, not just read. It references the Toolkit worksheets by number; print them (each has its own print button, and the whole book prints cleanly per section), complete them against your actual household, and file them in one place (the fire-proof if you have it, the shared drive if you don't, the “the folder” that's the Plan's home). The annual review at the end is the loop that makes the rest of the book's preparation a state instead of a memory.

The readiness consolidation — the ten items

  1. Household risk assessment — Worksheet 6 (the hazard inspection) + the Ch. 27 local hazard list. The output: the ranked list of “the top three risks to this household, specifically”, which sizes everything that follows (the kit's emphasis, the plan's emphasis, the training's priority).
  2. First-aid kit inventory — Worksheet 4 (the shopping check) + Worksheet 5 (the inspection log). The output: the kit that's stocked, dated, and on the monthly check — and the gap list that's the one shopping trip.
  3. Emergency contact sheet — Worksheet 3, localised (the verified local numbers, the out-of-area contact, the per-member lines). The output: the page on the fridge and in every phone.
  4. Family evacuation plan — Worksheets 12 & 13 (the plan and the meeting points) + the Ch. 28 four pages. The output: the drilled plan, with the date of the last drill written on it (the date is the proof).
  5. Essential medication checklist — Worksheet 15, per member, with the 72-hour go-bag rule (Ch. 29) and the backup-supply location. The output: the medication that's found in the dark, in the event, by the person who's not the member.
  6. Training & certification record — Worksheet 23. The output: the dated record of the household's first-aid and CPR certifications, with the expiry as the calendar entry (the refresher that's booked before the lapse, Ch. 26's cycle).
  7. Vehicle safety checklist — Worksheet 10. The output: the vehicle kit and the roadside sequence (Ch. 24) that's the trip-ready state, checked at the service.
  8. Workplace & school check — Worksheet 9 + the Ch. 25 questions (the “where's the AED, who's the first-aider, where's the assembly point” asked at the workspace and confirmed at the school). The output: the answered questions, written down, because the answer that's asked-and-forgotten is the answer that's absent in the event.
  9. Emergency supply inspection — Worksheet 14 (the go-bag) + the Ch. 29 rotation. The output: the bag that's passed the grab test, with the rotation date and the next-swap list.
  10. Disaster readiness — Worksheet 16, against the local list (Ch. 27). The output: the per-hazard preparedness that's the deep-prep for the region's actual risks, and the official-channel subscription confirmed (the app installed, the alert on).

The personal goals — the next twelve months

The readiness plan that's only the checklists is the readiness plan that's static; the goals are the delta. The common first-year set, sized to the household's actual gaps from the ten items above:

The capability goals

The adults' first-aid + CPR course (the one that's the Ch. 6/7/8 concepts turned into the skill) — the highest-weight goal in the book, and the one the rest of the preparation is in service of; the refresher date booked; and the “the second adult is current” if the household's single-current-adult is the single-point-of-failure the assessment found.

The environment goals

The top-three risks from the risk assessment, each with the specific fix and the date (the anchored furniture, the CO detector, the bathroom grip, the water-heater temperature) — the environment goals are the Ch. 21–25 chapters' output as the dated commitments, and the dated commitment is what separates the goal from the intention.

The family goals

The drill run (the Ch. 28 yearly, with the debrief), the children's readiness at their level (the number, the meeting point, the “stop-drop-roll” — Ch. 22), and the vulnerable-member's plan confirmed (the check-in network live, the older adult's clinical review done, Ch. 23) — the family goals are the plan's members becoming the plan's capability.

The knowledge goals

The local numbers verified and speed-dialed, the official disaster channel subscribed, the poison centre found and written (the front-matter guide, completed for the region), and the “the household can recite the roadmap” (the Ch. front-matter's six steps, said by the non-expert member, which is the test that the knowledge is the household's and not the reader's) — the knowledge goals are the information layer that the other three goals run on.

The annual review — Worksheet 25, the loop

Once a year (the anniversary of this review, on the calendar, non-negotiable, the same discipline as the alarm test), the household runs Worksheet 25: each of the ten items re-checked against its current state, the expiry and the date columns scanned (the certification's lapse, the kit's expiry, the bag's rotation, the plan's last-drill date), the goals' completion marked, and the next year's goals set from the gaps. The review is an hour, at the table, with the printed worksheets, and it produces the two numbers the household keeps: the readiness score (the items current out of the ten — the trend line that's the motivation) and the top-three next-year goals (the delta). The household that runs the loop is the household where the preparedness is the state; the household that doesn't is the household where it's the memory, and the memory is what the event erases first.

✓The closing standard — the book's promise, kept

Recognise danger (Parts I–IV gave you the patterns). Act safely (the scene-safety and the competence boundary ran through every chapter). Know what to do (the sequences, the drills, the trained skill). Prevent the avoidable (Parts V's checklists, now dated and owned). Prepare before the emergency (this plan, now reviewed annually). The book's final instruction is the first one, restated: in the real emergency, the call is the action — everything here is what you do around the call, before it, and long before it.

Key takeaways

  • The ten consolidation items map the book's worksheets to the household's actual state — each produces a dated, filed output, and the gaps produce the next trip and the next booking.
  • The capability goal (the hands-on course and the current refresher) outranks the rest — the preparation is in service of the trained pair of hands.
  • The dated commitment is the goal; the undated intention is the list. Every fix, drill and review carries the date that makes it real.
  • The annual review (Worksheet 25) is the loop: the ten items re-checked, the score kept, the next three goals set — the preparedness as the state, not the memory.
Review questions
  1. Why does the book put the hands-on course as the highest-weight personal goal, above the kit and the plan?
  2. What are the two numbers the annual review keeps, and why the trend rather than the single score?
  3. What does “the dated commitment is the goal” change about how the household tracks the preparation?

1) Because every other item in the plan is an asset that the trained hand operates — the kit is the supply, the plan is the script, the bag is the bridge, and the untrained household has the assets without the operator, which is the false-readiness the book's limitation statement (front matter) exists to prevent; the course is the conversion of the preparation from the potential into the capability, and it's the item whose absence invalidates the rest. 2) The readiness score (the current items out of the ten) and the top-three next-year goals; the trend rather than the single score because the single year's number is the snapshot (the 8/10 that's the comfort or the 6/10 that's the alarm) while the trend is the direction (the 6→7→8 that's the working loop, or the 8→8→8 that's the stalling one), and the direction is what the next year's goals should respond to — the preparation that's improving is the preparation that's alive, and the trend is the liveness made visible. 3) It converts the preparation from the attribute (“we're a prepared household”) into the obligation with the deadline (“the CO detector is fitted by X, the drill is run by Y, the refresher is booked by Z”), which is what makes it trackable (the done/not-done against the date), what makes it delegable (the member who owns the date), and what makes the annual review the verification instead of the invention — the date is the evidence the preparation happened, and the review checks the evidence.

Poison-related emergency support

India does not currently operate a single national 24/7 poison-control hotline equivalent to the US 1-800-222-1222.
